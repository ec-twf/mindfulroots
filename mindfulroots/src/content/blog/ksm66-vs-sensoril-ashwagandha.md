KSM-66 and Sensoril are both standardised ashwagandha extracts, and the difference comes down to how they are made and dosed. KSM-66 is a root-only extract with the deepest set of human trials, which makes it the sensible default for daytime stress and general use. Sensoril is a root-and-leaf extract, standardised to a higher withanolide percentage and taken at a lower dose, more often formulated for calm and sleep. Neither wins outright; your goal decides. You can read more on the [ashwagandha page](/products/ashwagandha/), and the [stress guide](/guides/stress/) sets it among other daily options.

## The verdict, before the detail

Choose by goal, not by brand prestige. If you want support for daytime stress, focus, and general resilience, KSM-66 is the safer starting point because most of the well-known trials used it, so you are matching a real studied dose. If your main goal is evening wind-down and sleep, Sensoril's higher standardisation and lower dose are commonly built into calming formulas, though a KSM-66 product taken at night does the same job. What matters more than the trademark is a stated dose, a single-ingredient product, and taking it every day.

## What each extract actually is

Ashwagandha (*Withania somnifera*) is an adaptogenic herb whose activity comes from compounds called withanolides. KSM-66 and Sensoril are two ways of concentrating those compounds. KSM-66 is drawn from the root only, using a water-based process, and is standardised to at least 5% withanolides; its typical studied dose is 300 to 600 mg per day. Sensoril uses both root and leaf, is standardised to at least 10% withanolide glycosides, and is taken at a lower dose of roughly 125 to 250 mg per day. The higher percentage does not mean "stronger" in a simple way, because the dose is lower to match; it means a different concentration-and-dose design.

## What the evidence says

The evidence base is uneven between the two, and that is the most decision-relevant fact. KSM-66 anchors most of the widely cited human research. A trial by [Chandrasekhar and colleagues (2012)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23439798/) found that a standardised root extract reduced perceived stress and serum cortisol over 60 days, and a later study by [Salve and colleagues (2019)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32021735/) reported similar cortisol reductions. For sleep specifically, [Langade and colleagues (2019)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31728244/) found improved sleep measures with a root extract over eight weeks. Sensoril has supportive data too, but a smaller published trial base, so claims about it should be read more cautiously. The [NCCIH ashwagandha overview](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ashwagandha) is a good plain-language summary of where the evidence stands overall, which is promising but still developing.

## Which ashwagandha extract to buy for sleep vs stress

Pick the extract by what you actually want from it, then check the same short list of specs either way:

- **For daytime stress and focus:** a KSM-66 root extract at 300 to 600 mg per day, matching the trial range and the deepest evidence base.
- **For evening calm and sleep:** a Sensoril product at 125 to 250 mg, or a KSM-66 product taken at night; both are reasonable, so choose on tolerance and cost.
- **In either case:** a stated withanolide percentage, a single-ingredient formula with no added stimulants, and third-party testing for identity, potency, and contaminants.

## When the premium is not worth it

Every page ranking for these brand terms is published by someone selling one of the extracts, so it is worth hearing this from a site that sells neither: the branded name is not magic. What you are paying for is the assurance that the dose and standardisation match what was tested. A generic ashwagandha that clearly states its withanolide percentage and uses a studied dose can be just as good and cheaper. The premium is worth paying when a product gives you no standardisation figure at all, because then you have no idea what you are taking. It stops being worth it the moment a well-specified generic matches the numbers on the branded label. Do not pay for a trademark that is standing in for information a cheaper product already gives you.

## Cautions and interactions

Ashwagandha is generally well tolerated but not for everyone, and the cautions apply to both extracts. Avoid it in pregnancy, and be careful with thyroid conditions because it can affect thyroid hormone levels. If you take sedatives, thyroid medication, immunosuppressants, or diabetes or blood-pressure medication, the potential for interaction is real, and rare cases of liver injury have been reported with ashwagandha products. It supports the stress response; it does not treat anxiety, depression, or any diagnosed condition. Talk to your prescriber or doctor before starting if you take medication, have a health condition, or are pregnant or breastfeeding.
