Passionflower and chamomile are two traditional calming herbs, both used in teas and supplements to ease mild anxiety and help the body wind down. Neither is a sedative drug, and neither is going to overpower a genuinely stressful day, so the honest framing is that you are choosing between two gentle options with different evidence and different best uses. If you want the quick version: passionflower has been studied for short bursts of situational tension, while chamomile has the stronger record for steady, everyday anxiety over several weeks.

## The verdict, before the detail

Reach for **passionflower** when the problem is a specific, time-limited spike of nerves — the evening before a presentation, say. Its best-known trial, by [Movafegh and colleagues (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1213/ane.0b013e31815ee61c), gave patients an oral passionflower preparation before surgery and found it lowered pre-operative anxiety without heavy sedation.

Reach for **chamomile** when you want a slower, daily habit aimed at background anxiety. The standout study here, by [Amsterdam and colleagues (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1097/JCP.0b013e3181ac935c), gave a standardised chamomile extract to people with generalised anxiety disorder and saw a modest but real reduction in symptoms over eight weeks.

Neither result is a blockbuster, and both trials were small. That is the level the evidence sits at: promising, gentle, worth trying for mild symptoms, not a substitute for care.

## How they work in the body

Both herbs are thought to act partly through the GABA system, the brain's main calming signal, which is also where prescription anxiety drugs work, though far more weakly here. Chamomile contains apigenin, a flavonoid that binds loosely to benzodiazepine-type receptors. Passionflower's active constituents are less pinned down, but its extracts show similar GABA-related activity in laboratory work. The practical upshot is a light take-the-edge-off effect rather than the strong sedation of a drug.

## Head to head

| Feature | Passionflower | Chamomile |
|---|---|---|
| Best-studied use | Short-term, situational anxiety | Ongoing generalised anxiety |
| Onset | Fairly quick (within hours) | Builds over weeks for anxiety |
| Typical form | Tea, tincture, capsule | Tea, standardised extract |
| Sleep evidence | Small tea study, modest benefit | Traditional, thin trial data |
| Main caution | Sedative stacking, pre-surgery | Ragweed-family allergy |

For sleep specifically, passionflower has a small edge in the published record: a study by [Ngan and Conduit (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1002/ptr.3400) found a nightly passionflower tea produced a modest improvement in subjective sleep quality in healthy adults. A longer chamomile trial by [Mao and colleagues (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.phymed.2016.10.012) looked at long-term use for generalised anxiety and found it well tolerated, with a signal toward fewer relapses. The [NCCIH chamomile overview](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/chamomile) and the [Memorial Sloan Kettering passionflower monograph](https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/passionflower) are useful, sober references if you want the safety detail.

## Cautions and interactions

Both herbs are gentle, but gentle is not the same as inert. Passionflower can add to the effect of sedatives and is best avoided in the run-up to surgery or anaesthesia. Chamomile belongs to the daisy family, so anyone allergic to ragweed, marigold, or related plants may react, and high intakes have a theoretical interaction with blood thinners. Pregnancy is a reason to be cautious with both. As always, if you take prescription medication or have a diagnosed anxiety condition, treat these as support to discuss with a clinician rather than a self-directed fix.

If you take any prescription medication, especially sedatives or anticoagulants, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting either herb.
