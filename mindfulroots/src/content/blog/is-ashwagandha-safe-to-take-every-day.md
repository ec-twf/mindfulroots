For most healthy adults, taking ashwagandha every day is well tolerated for up to about two to three months, which is the length of most trials. Beyond that, long-term daily safety is simply less studied, so ongoing use is best paired with a studied dose and occasional monitoring. Some people are better off not taking it daily at all. Ashwagandha (*Withania somnifera*) is an adaptogenic herb used in Ayurvedic practice to support the body's stress response. The reason "every day" is the common question is that the benefit builds with consistent use (the stress and cortisol trials dosed it daily for weeks), so people naturally ask how long that daily habit can safely run.

## What daily-use trials actually found

The strongest evidence for daily use comes from short randomised trials. A placebo-controlled study by [Chandrasekhar and colleagues (2012)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23439798/) dosed a root extract daily for 60 days and reported lower perceived stress and cortisol with only mild side effects. An eight-week KSM-66 trial by [Lopresti and colleagues (2019)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31517876/) found similar daily-use benefits on stress and sleep with good tolerability. A safety and effectiveness review by [Tandon and Yadav (2020)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32305638/) concluded that across controlled trials adverse effects were mostly mild and transient. The consistent limitation is duration: these trials run weeks to a few months, so daily use *within* that window is well supported, while continuous use for a year or more is largely unstudied.

## The risks that matter for daily use

Three effects justify caution with an everyday habit. First, **liver injury**: a case series by [Björnsson and colleagues (2020)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31766537/) described previously healthy people who developed liver injury after taking ashwagandha, all recovering after stopping, and the NIH [LiverTox database](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK548536/) now lists it as an uncommon cause of drug-induced liver injury. Stop and seek care for yellowing skin or eyes, dark urine, or right-sided abdominal pain. Second, **thyroid effects**: ashwagandha can raise T3 and T4, a real problem for anyone with an overactive or medicated thyroid taken daily. Third, **sedation and immune stimulation**, which matter more the longer and more consistently the herb is used. None is common, but daily use is exactly the pattern where a rare effect has the most chances to appear.

## What to look for in an ashwagandha supplement for long-term daily use

If you are going to take it daily, the product choice matters more, not less. Favour a **single-ingredient, root-only standardised extract** (KSM-66 and Sensoril are the most studied) at the trial-backed 300–600 mg a day, standardised to a stated withanolide percentage so each dose is consistent. Insist on **third-party testing** for identity, potency, and contaminants such as heavy metals, since daily exposure raises the stakes on purity. Avoid mega-dose "stress blends" that stack stimulants or sedatives you cannot dose separately, and steer clear of products that do not state their extract or dose. ## Who should not take it daily

Some people should not make ashwagandha a daily habit at all: anyone pregnant, and with caution while breastfeeding; people with an overactive or medicated thyroid; those with autoimmune conditions, liver disease, or an upcoming surgery. It can add to the effect of sedatives, thyroid drugs, and immunosuppressants. If any of these apply, daily ashwagandha is one to skip or to use only under a clinician's guidance. For everyone else, a studied dose, a purity-tested product, and occasional check-ins keep daily use close to what the evidence supports. Talk to your prescriber before starting daily ashwagandha, especially alongside any regular medication.
