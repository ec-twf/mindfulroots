For everyday stress, ashwagandha has the deeper and more consistent human evidence, so it is the sensible first pick if you want a single adaptogen with real trial support behind it. Holy basil, known in Ayurveda as tulsi, is a fair choice for mild, day-to-day tension and carries extra evidence for metabolic markers, but its mood studies are smaller and less controlled. These are two different plants with two different track records, not one ingredient under two labels.

| | Holy basil (tulsi) | Ashwagandha |
|---|---|---|
| Plant | *Ocimum sanctum* leaf | *Withania somnifera* root |
| Best for | Mild daily tension, metabolic support | General stress, cortisol, sleep onset |
| Human evidence | Smaller, mixed trials | Larger, more RCTs |
| Typical study dose | ~300–600 mg extract daily | 240–600 mg extract daily |
| Onset | Weeks of daily use | Weeks of daily use |
| Main caution | Blood sugar, possible fertility effects | Thyroid, sedation, autoimmune conditions |

## How each one works

An adaptogen is a plant thought to help the body cope with stress by nudging the stress-response system back toward baseline rather than stimulating or sedating it directly. Ashwagandha is the root of *Withania somnifera*, and its active withanolides are studied mainly for calming the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, the loop that governs cortisol. Holy basil is the leaf of *Ocimum sanctum*, rich in eugenol and ursolic acid, and its research spreads across stress, blood sugar, and blood pressure rather than concentrating on mood.

That difference in research focus matters when you choose. Ashwagandha has been pointed squarely at stress and anxiety endpoints. Holy basil has been studied as a broader metabolic-and-stress herb, so the stress signal is real but less isolated.

## What the evidence says

A 2014 systematic review of human ashwagandha trials found that most reported reductions in anxiety and stress scores, with a good safety record across the studies examined ([Pratte et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1089/acm.2014.0177)). A later randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial using a standardised root extract reported lower stress ratings and reduced morning cortisol after 60 days ([Lopresti et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1097/MD.0000000000017186)). The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health notes that short-term use appears generally safe while flagging gaps in long-term data ([NCCIH: Ashwagandha](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ashwagandha)).

Holy basil's human record is real but thinner. A 2017 systematic review of tulsi trials found favourable results across metabolic and psychological stress outcomes with no significant adverse events reported, while noting that the studies were small and varied in quality ([Jamshidi & Cohen, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1155/2017/9217567)). In short, ashwagandha has more and better-controlled trials aimed at stress; holy basil has fewer, broader ones.

## What to look for in a holy basil supplement for stress

If holy basil is the herb you want, the label tells you most of what you need. Check that it names the species (*Ocimum sanctum* or *Ocimum tenuiflorum*) and the plant part, states a per-serving dose in the roughly 300–600 mg extract range used in trials, and ideally standardises to a marker such as ursolic acid so you know the extract is consistent batch to batch. Third-party testing for identity, heavy metals, and microbial contamination matters more for a leaf product than most buyers realise. Skip proprietary blends that pool several herbs behind one total weight, because you cannot tell how much tulsi you are actually getting.

Choose the herb that fits your goal and tolerance, not the one with the busier label.

## Cautions and interactions

Both herbs are support for everyday stress, not a treatment for a diagnosed anxiety or mood disorder. Holy basil may lower blood sugar, so anyone on diabetes medication should monitor closely, and animal data raise a question about fertility that argues for caution if you are trying to conceive. Ashwagandha can interact with thyroid medication, may add to the effect of sedatives, and is usually avoided in autoimmune thyroid conditions and pregnancy. If you take prescription medication or have an ongoing health condition, talk to your prescriber before starting either one, and stop if you notice a reaction.

If your stress is persistent or affecting daily life, a supplement is not the right first step. See [get help](/get-help/) for support options.
