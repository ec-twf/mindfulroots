If you are choosing between "ashwagandha" and "KSM-66," the short answer is that they are not two different things. KSM-66 is a branded, standardised extract of ashwagandha, so the real choice is between a generic ashwagandha of unknown potency and one specific, well-documented version of the same plant. For most people who want to match what the research tested, a named standardised extract like KSM-66 is the more reliable pick, while a good generic root extract can still work if the label is transparent about dose and standardisation.

Because this is a labelling distinction rather than a botanical rivalry, the useful comparison is about consistency and evidence, not about one herb beating another. Our [ashwagandha page](/products/ashwagandha/) and the [stress guide](/guides/stress/) cover where the whole ingredient fits before you get to brand names.

## Same plant, different label

Ashwagandha (*Withania somnifera*) is an adaptogenic herb used in Ayurvedic practice to support the body's response to stress. KSM-66 is a trademarked extract of that same herb, made from the root only and standardised to a withanolide content of at least 5 percent. Withanolides are the compounds most linked to ashwagandha's calming and stress-buffering effects, which is why a stated percentage matters more than the word "ashwagandha" on its own.

The reason KSM-66 comes up so often is that it appears in a good share of the human trials. A randomised study by [Chandrasekhar and colleagues (2012)](https://doi.org/10.4103/0253-7176.106022) using a high-concentration root extract found reduced stress and lower cortisol over 60 days. Later trials by [Salve and colleagues (2019)](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.6466) and [Lopresti and colleagues (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1097/MD.0000000000017186) reported similar stress and sleep benefits with standardised root extract. When you buy the same standardised form used in those trials, you are closer to the conditions that produced the results.

## How they compare

| | Generic ashwagandha | KSM-66 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | The whole herb, powder or extract | Branded root-only extract of the same herb |
| Standardisation | Varies, sometimes unstated | At least 5 percent withanolides, HPLC |
| Trial record | Mixed; depends on the product | Used in several published RCTs |
| Best for | Budget use with a transparent label | Matching what the studies tested |

The table makes the honest point: the gap is consistency, not a secret extra ingredient. A generic extract that clearly states its withanolide percentage and dose can be a fine choice. The risk with an unlabelled generic is that potency drifts between batches, so you cannot be sure your dose resembles anything that was studied.

## What to look for in a KSM-66 ashwagandha supplement

If you want the form most trials used, the label should do the work for you. Check that it names KSM-66 specifically rather than only saying "ashwagandha extract," and that it gives the dose per serving. Trials clustered around 300 mg taken twice daily or a single 600 mg dose, so a product in that range lets you follow the evidence rather than guess. Confirm it is standardised to at least 5 percent withanolides, since that is the compound class doing the work, and prioritise third-party testing for purity, because ashwagandha root can carry contaminants if sourcing is poor. Our [ashwagandha page](/products/ashwagandha/) lays out the evidence and typical use, and the [stress guide](/guides/stress/) shows how it sits beside other stress supports. A clear dose and a stated standardisation percentage tell you more than any "premium" claim on the front.

## Cautions

Ashwagandha is generally well tolerated, but it is not for everyone. It can add to the effect of sedatives and thyroid medication, and there have been rare reports of liver issues, so anyone with a thyroid or liver condition should be cautious. Avoid it in pregnancy and breastfeeding. If you take prescription medication or manage a health condition, check with your prescriber or pharmacist before starting, whichever extract you choose. Ashwagandha supports the stress response; it does not treat an anxiety or mood disorder.
