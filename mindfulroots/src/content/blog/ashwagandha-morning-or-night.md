Ashwagandha works taken in the morning or at night, and the more important thing is taking it every day. Pick night if your main goal is sleep and evening wind-down, and morning if you want support for daytime stress and focus. Because ashwagandha builds up gradually over weeks rather than acting like a fast sedative, the time of day is a preference, not a rule. You can read more on the [ashwagandha page](/products/ashwagandha/), and the [stress guide](/guides/stress/) sets it among other options for daily stress support.

## Why timing matters less than consistency

Ashwagandha (*Withania somnifera*) is an adaptogenic herb whose activity comes from a group of compounds called withanolides. Unlike caffeine or a sedative, it does not produce an obvious same-hour effect you can feel and time around. Instead, its benefits for the stress response accumulate with regular use. In the trials that reported reduced stress and lower cortisol, participants took it daily for weeks, and the endpoints were measured at four, eight, or twelve weeks rather than hours after a dose. That is the single most useful fact for deciding when to take it: because the effect is cumulative, missing days matters more than choosing the perfect hour. Attach it to something you already do every day, morning coffee or brushing your teeth at night, so you actually take it consistently.

## When night makes sense

Evening is a sensible default if your reason for taking ashwagandha is sleep or winding down. A randomised controlled trial by [Langade and colleagues (2019)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31728244/) found that a standardised root extract improved several sleep measures over eight weeks, and many people simply find the calmer stress response it supports fits the hours before bed. The species name *somnifera* even means "sleep-inducing", though in practice the effect is supportive rather than sedative. If you notice you feel relaxed after a dose, night is the natural home for it, and taking it after your evening meal also helps with tolerability.

## When morning makes sense

If your goal is daytime stress resilience or focus, morning works just as well. The stress and cortisol trials that anchor ashwagandha's reputation were not about sleep at all. A frequently cited study by [Chandrasekhar and colleagues (2012)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23439798/) found that a standardised root extract reduced perceived stress and serum cortisol over 60 days, and a later trial by [Salve and colleagues (2019)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32021735/) reported similar cortisol reductions. Since cortisol naturally peaks in the morning, some people prefer a morning dose to support that part of the daily rhythm. The [NCCIH ashwagandha overview](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ashwagandha) is a good plain-language reference on the current state of the evidence. A split dose, part morning and part evening, is also well supported and keeps levels steadier.

## What to look for in an ashwagandha supplement for nighttime stress

If you are choosing a product specifically for evening use, these criteria matter more than the time on the clock:

- **A standardised root extract**, such as KSM-66, or a label that states a withanolide percentage, so the dose matches the studies.
- **A dose in the studied range**, roughly 300 to 600 mg per day of extract, rather than an unspecified "proprietary blend".
- **Third-party testing** for identity, potency, and contaminants.
- **A single-ingredient formulation** with no added stimulants, so it suits wind-down and lets you attribute any effect.

## Cautions and interactions

Ashwagandha is generally well tolerated, but it is not for everyone. It should be avoided in pregnancy, and people with thyroid conditions should be cautious because it can affect thyroid hormone levels. If you take sedatives, thyroid medication, immunosuppressants, or diabetes or blood-pressure medication, the potential for interaction is real, and rare cases of liver injury have been reported with ashwagandha products. It supports the stress response; it does not treat anxiety, depression, or any diagnosed condition. Talk to your prescriber or doctor before starting if you take medication, have a health condition, or are pregnant or breastfeeding.
