For most people, 5-HTP is best taken in the evening, with a light snack rather than on a completely empty stomach. It has a short half-life and its serotonin-linked effect tends to feel calming, which fits wind-down and sleep. A morning dose can make sense if you are using it for daytime mood support and it does not make you drowsy, but night is the sensible default. Start low, keep the time consistent, and read the safety note below carefully. Our [5-HTP hub](/products/5-htp/) covers the evidence and the crucial interaction caution, and the [sleep guide](/guides/sleep/) sets it among other evening options.

## Why evening is the default

The timing question really turns on two things: how long 5-HTP stays active, and how its effect feels. 5-HTP (5-hydroxytryptophan) is absorbed fairly quickly and has a relatively short half-life, so it does its work over a matter of hours rather than lingering all day. As [Turner and colleagues (2006)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pharmthera.2005.06.004) describe in their pharmacology review, 5-HTP crosses into the brain readily and is converted into serotonin there. Because serotonin is also the precursor to melatonin, the evening is a natural fit: the calming, sleep-adjacent side of its effect lands when you want it. For someone taking 5-HTP for sleep or evening mood support, a dose 30 to 60 minutes before bed is a reasonable, if not precisely studied, window.

## When a morning dose makes sense

Morning is not wrong, it is just narrower in use. If your reason for taking 5-HTP is daytime mood support and you have found that it does not make you drowsy, a morning dose spreads the effect across the waking hours when you want it. A review by [Birdsall (1998)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9727088/) summarising 5-HTP's use as a serotonin precursor notes its role in mood and appetite regulation, both of which are daytime concerns for some people. The deciding factor is personal: if 5-HTP relaxes you, keep it at night; if it feels neutral or you specifically want daytime support, morning is fine. Consistency matters more than the exact hour.

## Food, dose, and getting started

The most common complaint about 5-HTP is nausea, and a light snack is the simplest fix. A small carbohydrate-based snack also gives a theoretical absorption edge, since a very large protein-heavy meal can compete for the same transport into the brain, though this is a minor consideration in practice. On dose, the evidence-honest framing is that 5-HTP sits at an emerging tier for mood: a broad review by [Maffei (2020)](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms22010181) found it biologically plausible with some supportive human data but a clear need for better trials. Start at the low end, typically 50 mg, and hold there before considering more.

## What to look for in a 5-HTP supplement for nighttime use

If you are choosing 5-HTP specifically for the evening, these criteria matter more than the clock:

- **A stated 5-HTP dose per capsule**, usually 50 to 100 mg, so you can start low, not a vague "proprietary blend".
- **Sourced from *Griffonia simplicifolia* seed extract**, the standard natural source.
- **No added serotonergic ingredients** (such as St John's wort), which would compound the interaction risk.
- **Third-party testing** for identity, potency, and contaminants; some products add vitamin B6, the cofactor for the conversion step.

## Cautions and interactions

This is the part that matters most for 5-HTP. Because it raises serotonin, combining 5-HTP with SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs, triptans, tramadol, or other serotonergic drugs can raise the risk of serotonin syndrome, a rare but serious reaction. The [Memorial Sloan Kettering 5-HTP monograph](https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/5-htp) sets out these interaction cautions. Do not combine 5-HTP with any mood, migraine, or pain medication without medical supervision, and do not increase the dose on your own. 5-HTP supports mood and sleep; it does not treat depression, anxiety, or any diagnosed condition. Talk to your prescriber or doctor before starting if you take medication, have a health condition, or are pregnant or breastfeeding.
