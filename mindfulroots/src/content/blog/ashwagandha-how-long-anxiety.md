If you have just started ashwagandha for anxiety, give it 4 to 8 weeks before judging it. This is not a fast calming pill you feel within an hour. It works gradually, and the trials that lowered stress and anxiety scores ran for about 60 days. A minority of people feel a little steadier within the first week or two, but the real test is roughly two months of consistent daily use.

## Why ashwagandha is a slow build

Ashwagandha is classed as an adaptogen, a plant traditionally used to help the body cope with stress. In practical terms, it seems to nudge the stress-hormone system over time rather than switching off tension on the spot. That is why the effect creeps up on you instead of arriving with the first dose.

The active compounds are a group called withanolides, concentrated in standardized root extracts. Rather than producing an immediate sedative hit, they appear to influence the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, the system that governs cortisol, across weeks of daily intake. So the honest framing is repetition, not a single dose: you are asking a stress-regulation system to settle, and that takes time. Ashwagandha sits in the moderate-evidence tier for stress and anxiety support, so treat it as support rather than a treatment for an anxiety disorder. The [National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ashwagandha) notes that studies are mostly short and small, which is worth keeping in mind.

## What the trials show about timing

The clearest evidence comes from 60-day studies. A frequently cited randomized, placebo-controlled trial by [Chandrasekhar and colleagues (2012)](https://doi.org/10.4103/0253-7176.106022) used a high-concentration root extract over 60 days and reported lower stress scores and reduced serum cortisol compared with placebo. A later randomized trial by [Lopresti and colleagues (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1097/MD.0000000000017186) also ran 60 days and found reduced stress and morning cortisol, again measured at the end of that window rather than in the first days.

A shorter look comes from [Salve and colleagues (2019)](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.6466), an 8-week randomized study in healthy adults that measured improvements in stress and sleep with assessments at both 4 and 8 weeks, with the effect more established by week 8. Pulling these together, a [2014 systematic review by Pratte and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1089/acm.2014.0177) concluded that ashwagandha showed benefit for anxiety and stress across human trials, while noting the studies were small and varied. The common thread across all of them is the clock: benefits were counted in weeks, and 8 weeks is the mark that keeps recurring.

## A realistic timeline

| Time on a daily dose | What is happening | What you might notice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Week 1 to 2 | Withanolides building up | Some feel slightly calmer or sleep better |
| Week 3 to 4 | Stress response beginning to settle | Early, modest change for many |
| Week 6 to 8 | Effect most established in trials | Lower day-to-day tension and cortisol |
| After 8 weeks | Fair point to assess | Decide whether to continue or stop |

Take it at the same time each day and stick with it. Skipping days or jumping the dose up early tends to muddy your read on whether it is actually helping.

## What to look for in an ashwagandha supplement for anxiety relief

The best ashwagandha supplement for anxiety relief is a standardized root extract at a dose that matches the trials. A few criteria separate a useful product from a weak one:

- **Standardized extract:** look for a named, standardized root extract such as KSM-66 or Sensoril, with the withanolide percentage stated on the label.
- **Root, not leaf:** most of the strong stress-and-anxiety trials used root extracts; be cautious with cheap products that lean on leaf material.
- **A trial-backed dose:** studies commonly used around 300 mg twice daily of a concentrated root extract; match that ballpark rather than guessing.
- **Third-party testing:** independent verification confirms the extract and dose, which matters more here because standardization varies widely.

## Cautions and interactions

Ashwagandha is generally well tolerated over the short trial periods studied, but a few cautions matter. It can raise thyroid hormone levels, so people with thyroid conditions or on thyroid medication should check with a clinician. There are rare reports of liver injury, so stop and seek advice if you develop symptoms like unusual fatigue, dark urine, or yellowing skin. It may add to the effect of sedatives and blood-pressure or blood-sugar medications. It is not recommended in pregnancy, and people with autoimmune conditions should be cautious because it can stimulate immune activity.
