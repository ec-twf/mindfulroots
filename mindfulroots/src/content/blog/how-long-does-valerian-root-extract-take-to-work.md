How long valerian takes to work depends on what you are asking of it. For a single night, some people feel mild drowsiness within about one to two hours of a dose taken before bed — but many feel little on the first try. The more dependable benefit in trials is one that builds over **two to four weeks** of consistent nightly use, so one restless night is not a fair test. Expect a possible gentle same-night effect, but judge valerian for ongoing sleep only after a couple of weeks. ## The two timelines: one dose versus built-up benefit

Valerian (*Valeriana officinalis*) is a root used for centuries to ease restlessness and support sleep, and its constituents — valerenic acid chief among them — are thought to act on the GABA system, the same calming pathway many sleep aids target. That mechanism can produce a mild, same-night drowsiness in some people within one to two hours of a dose. But the acute effect is inconsistent from person to person, which is exactly why the research leans on repeated dosing. The [NCCIH profile of valerian](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/valerian) notes that evidence for its sleep benefit is mixed and that it is typically taken nightly rather than as a one-off, reflecting how it has been studied.

## Why the fuller benefit takes two to four weeks

The trial data point to accumulation. A [systematic review and meta-analysis by Bent and colleagues (2006)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17145239/) pooled 16 studies and found valerian may improve subjective sleep quality, while cautioning that the trials were methodologically variable — and much of that work used nightly dosing sustained across weeks rather than a single administration. A separate [meta-analysis by Fernández-San-Martín and colleagues (2010)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20347389/) reached a similarly qualified conclusion, again from studies of repeated nightly use. The practical read, echoed on [Examine's valerian page](https://examine.com/supplements/valerian/): treat valerian as something you give a fair multi-week trial, not a knock-out taken on demand. If you test it for one night and quit, you are testing it on the timeline where it looks weakest.

## What changes the speed

Two variables move the timeline more than anything else. The first is **dose and standardisation**: the trials used meaningful amounts of a standardised root extract, and a weak powder or a crowded "night blend" that under-doses the valerian will act slower or not at all. The second is **consistency** — the two-to-four-week benefit only accrues if you take it nightly, ideally at the same time. As a rough rule: a possible acute drowsiness the same night within one to two hours, and a full two-to-four-week trial before deciding whether valerian genuinely helps your sleep.

## What to look for in a valerian root extract supplement for sleep

To reproduce what the studies found, buy what they used: a **standardised extract**. Look for a product standardised to **valerenic acid**, with the dose per serving printed clearly so you can match a meaningful amount rather than a token sprinkle. **Third-party testing** (NSF, USP, or Informed Choice) is the other signal worth paying for. Single-ingredient extracts make the timeline predictable; multi-herb "sleep" blends often hide how much valerian you are actually getting, which is a common reason a product seems to do nothing. Choose on dose and testing, not on the loudest label.

## A note on safety and expectations

Valerian is generally well tolerated, but it can cause drowsiness, so avoid driving until you know how it affects you, and do not combine it with alcohol, sedatives, or other sleep medications without medical advice. If sleep problems are persistent or severe, or you take regular medication, speak to a doctor or pharmacist before starting. Valerian is a support for occasional restlessness, not a treatment for a diagnosed sleep disorder.
