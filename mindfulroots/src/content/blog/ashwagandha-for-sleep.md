Ashwagandha (Withania somnifera) is an adaptogenic root used in Ayurvedic tradition to help the body handle stress, standardised in modern supplements to its active withanolides. For sleep, the doses that actually showed results in trials sit in a narrow band: **300 mg of a standardised extract twice a day, or 600 mg once in the evening**, taken consistently for six to eight weeks. It is not a sedative and it will not knock you out on night one. Its benefit for sleep appears to work through a calmer stress-hormone curve, which is why the effect builds slowly.

## The dose the studies used

Almost every clinical trial on ashwagandha and sleep used a standardised root extract, not raw powder, and stayed within a tight dose range. The two most-studied branded forms are KSM-66 and Sensoril, which differ in how they are extracted and in their withanolide concentration.

A [2019 trial by Langade and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.5797) gave people with insomnia and anxiety 300 mg of a full-spectrum root extract twice daily for ten weeks. Sleep onset, sleep quality, and anxiety scores all improved against placebo. A follow-up [2021 study by the same group](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2020.113276) tested 600 mg per day and again found better sleep efficiency and quality, with clearer gains in people who already had insomnia than in healthy sleepers.

The pattern across trials is consistent: 300 to 600 mg of a standardised extract per day, given time to work. Higher is not obviously better, and most studies capped the daily dose around 600 mg.

## What the evidence shows

Pooled analysis backs up the individual trials. A [2021 systematic review and meta-analysis by Cheah and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0257843) combined five randomised trials and found ashwagandha produced a small-to-moderate improvement in overall sleep quality, with a stronger effect in people diagnosed with insomnia and at doses of 600 mg per day or more taken for eight weeks or longer.

| Study | Design | Dose | Key finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| Langade 2019 | RCT, 60 adults | 300 mg twice daily | Better sleep onset and quality |
| Langade 2021 | RCT, 80 adults | 600 mg daily | Improved sleep efficiency |
| Salve 2019 | RCT, 60 adults | 240–600 mg daily | Lower morning cortisol |
| Cheah 2021 | Meta-analysis, 5 RCTs | 120–600 mg daily | Modest overall sleep gain |

The cortisol thread ties it together. A [2019 trial by Salve and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.6466) found that a standardised extract lowered morning serum cortisol against placebo. Cortisol should fall in the evening to let sleep begin, and a stress response stuck in a high gear is a common reason people lie awake. A herb that blunts an over-active cortisol curve plausibly makes the wind-down easier, which fits the slow, cumulative sleep benefit the trials describe. This places ashwagandha at a moderate evidence tier for sleep support, stronger than most single herbs but not a replacement for treating a diagnosed sleep disorder.

## What to look for in an ashwagandha supplement for sleep

The dose only means something if the product is standardised, so read the label for the details that map to the trials. Look for a **root extract** (not leaf, not raw powder) that states its **withanolide percentage**, since that is the active fraction the studies standardised. Match the studied range: a product delivering **300 to 600 mg per day**, so you can reproduce a trial dose rather than guess. The two forms with the most sleep and stress data are **KSM-66 and Sensoril**, so a named standardised extract is a reasonable quality marker. Insist on **third-party testing**, because ashwagandha is sometimes adulterated or cut with leaf material. Skip proprietary "sleep blends" that bury the ashwagandha dose among other herbs, which makes it impossible to know what you are taking. ## Cautions and interactions

Ashwagandha is well tolerated in trials, but it is not for everyone. It can cause mild drowsiness, stomach upset, or loose stools, especially early on. Because it can lower cortisol and nudge thyroid hormone upward, people with thyroid conditions or those on thyroid medication should check with a clinician first. Rare reports of liver injury exist, so stop and seek advice if you notice yellowing skin, dark urine, or unusual fatigue.

Ashwagandha may add to the effect of sedatives, benzodiazepines, and other sleep medications, so combining them needs medical oversight. It can also interact with immunosuppressants and blood-sugar or blood-pressure drugs. Pregnancy and breastfeeding are firm reasons to avoid it, as high doses have traditionally been linked to pregnancy risk. The [National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ashwagandha) notes these safety limits and the need for more long-term data.

If persistent insomnia is your real problem, a supplement is not the first-line answer. Talk to your prescriber, and if low mood or anxiety is keeping you awake, our [get help](/get-help/) page points to genuine support.
