Search for how long any one supplement takes and you get a number for that supplement. Line up a dozen of those numbers and a more useful pattern appears: the timeline is set by what kind of thing the supplement is, and there are only three kinds. Once you know which group something belongs to, you know roughly how long to wait and, more usefully, when to stop waiting.

## The three clocks

An **acute active** works while it is in your blood: it acts on a receptor, you notice something, and the effect fades as the dose clears. L-theanine, L-tyrosine, passionflower, lemon balm and valerian all sit here, along with the sleepy side of 5-HTP and L-tryptophan. The window is 30 minutes to about 3 hours, so if two or three well-timed doses do nothing, more weeks will not change the answer.

A **repletion nutrient** has to refill a level before anything changes, so the clock belongs to your biology rather than to the pill. Magnesium and vitamin D are the two here. The practical consequence is counter-intuitive: how deficient you were matters more than how much you take, and someone who was not low to start with may get nothing at any dose.

An **adaptation supplement** gradually shifts a stress, mood or microbiome system. Ashwagandha, saffron, rhodiola, omega-3 and probiotics sit here, along with the mood side of 5-HTP and L-tryptophan. For this group the trial length is the timeline, because researchers settled on 8 or 12 weeks after shorter windows showed nothing. That also means the first week tells you very little.

## The acute group

L-theanine is the clearest case. Blood levels peak roughly 30 to 50 minutes after a dose, which matches when people report a relaxed, focused state arriving. The EEG work by [Kimura and colleagues (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsycho.2006.06.006) and the attention study by [Nobre and colleagues (2008)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18296328/) both measured effects inside a single session, which is why the [Examine summary](https://examine.com/supplements/theanine/) treats it as a same-session supplement rather than one that builds. Take it on an empty or lightly filled stomach and judge it that day.

L-tyrosine behaves the same way and is widely misunderstood as a daily build. If it does anything for focus it happens within 30 to 90 minutes of a dose taken before a demanding task, and mainly when you are stressed, sleep-deprived or under heavy mental load ([Colzato et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnbeh.2013.00200); reviews by [Jongkees et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpsychires.2015.08.014) and [Hase et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pbb.2015.02.024)). It does not accumulate the way an ADHD medication is titrated, the ADHD-specific evidence is thin, and early daily-use attempts suggest any effect fades. See the [focus guide](/guides/focus/).

Passionflower takes the edge off a stressful moment within 30 to 60 minutes, which is the window the pre-surgical anxiety trial by [Movafegh and colleagues (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1213/ane.0b013e31816d1c68) used. Its sleep and ongoing-anxiety benefit is slower, needing one to four weeks of daily use ([Akhondzadeh et al., 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11679026/); [Ngan & Conduit, 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21294203/); [MSKCC monograph](https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/passion-flower)).

Lemon balm produces a calming, mildly focusing effect within about one to three hours, which is when [Kennedy and colleagues (2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12062586/), [2004)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15272110/) measured mood and stress changes. For sleep and ongoing anxiety the benefit builds over roughly two weeks ([Cases et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22207903/); [Examine](https://examine.com/supplements/lemon-balm/)).

Valerian root may produce mild drowsiness within one to two hours of a bedtime dose, but the more reliable sleep benefit in trials builds over two to four weeks of consistent nightly use ([Bent et al., 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17145239/); meta-analysis by [Fernández-San-Martín et al., 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20347389/); [NCCIH](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/valerian)). One restless night is not a fair test of valerian, which is the trap in treating it as purely acute.

## The repletion group

Magnesium glycinate has two timelines, and conflating them is why people either overrate or dismiss it. A mild calming or sleep-easing effect can arrive the same evening, within an hour or two. The steadier benefit comes from topping up low magnesium in tissue, and that builds over about four to six weeks of consistent daily use. The anxiety and stress review by [Boyle and colleagues (2017)](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu9050429) and the trial work summarised by [Pickering and colleagues (2020)](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu12123672) both measured over weeks, not nights, and the [NIH ODS fact sheet](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Magnesium-HealthProfessional/) explains why: magnesium status is a pool, not a dose. For anxiety specifically, give it four to eight weeks, and expect the clearest result if you were low to begin with ([Tarleton et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0180067)).

Vitamin D3 is the slowest of the twelve, for a mechanical reason. The supplement raises 25(OH)D, that level climbs and then settles over roughly two to three months, and symptoms lag behind the number. The trials that reported a mood benefit checked in at 8 to 12 weeks, with the strongest effects in people who were low to begin with ([Jorde et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2796.2008.02008.x); [Spedding, 2014](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu6041501); [Vellekkatt & Menon, 2019](https://doi.org/10.4103/jpgm.JPGM_571_17)). The largest trial, [VITAL-DEP (Okereke et al., 2020)](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2020.10224), found no benefit in a population that was not deficient, which is worth knowing before you start counting weeks.

A bigger dose moves the blood number faster without necessarily moving symptoms faster. On the 60,000 IU weekly regimen used in some deficiency protocols, 25(OH)D starts rising within days and a deficiency is typically corrected over about six to eight weeks ([Ish-Shalom et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2008-1054)), while effects on mood, energy or muscle symptoms appear across one to three months. How low you started sets the timeline. See the [NIH ODS vitamin D fact sheet](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminD-HealthProfessional/) and the [vitamin D3 page](/products/vitamin-d3/).

## The adaptation group

Rhodiola rosea sits at the fast end of this group and also has an acute edge. A mild lift in energy can arrive within a few days, sometimes after a single dose in acute-fatigue studies ([Darbinyan et al., 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11081987/); [Olsson et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-0028-1088346)). The steadier effect on stress, mood and persistent tiredness is what the multi-week trials measured, so give it two to four weeks before judging ([Cropley et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1002/ptr.5486); systematic review by [Ishaque et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1186/1472-6882-12-70)). Take it in the morning, since the activating quality that helps fatigue also disturbs sleep late in the day.

Ashwagandha gives you nothing to feel early, which is what makes it hard to stick with. Plan on four to eight weeks of daily use. The trials that lowered perceived-stress scores and cortisol measured the change at around eight weeks: the 60-day study by [Chandrasekhar and colleagues (2012)](https://doi.org/10.4103/0253-7176.106022), the eight-week KSM-66 trial by [Lopresti and colleagues (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1097/MD.0000000000017186), and the work by [Salve and colleagues (2019)](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.6466), with the pattern summarised in the review by [Pratte and colleagues (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1089/acm.2014.0177) and the [NCCIH overview](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ashwagandha). A few people feel steadier in the first fortnight; the fair test is roughly two months.

Saffron extract needs six to eight weeks to judge. That is the window most mood trials used, though some studies of standardised extracts reported early signals around four weeks ([Kell et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ctim.2017.08.024); meta-analysis by [Marx et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1093/nutrit/nuz023); [MSKCC monograph](https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/saffron)). Give it a full course at a studied dose, around 28 to 30 mg a day, before deciding.

Probiotics split by outcome. Digestive changes, less bloating or more regular stools, often appear within a few days to two weeks. Anything running through the gut-brain chain takes longer, generally four to eight weeks in the trials that reported a benefit ([Messaoudi et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114510004319); meta-analysis by [Liu et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2019.03.023)). Two structural facts matter here: most strains are transient and largely cleared within one to two weeks of stopping, so they work only while you keep taking them, and colonisation is person-specific rather than guaranteed ([Zmora et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2018.08.041); [NIH ODS probiotics fact sheet](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Probiotics-HealthProfessional/)). If nothing has shifted after eight to twelve weeks on a named-strain product, the strain is probably not right for you.

Omega-3 fish oil works on a scale of weeks and months. Blood triglycerides start shifting in about four weeks, while mood and stress support usually needs eight to twelve weeks of consistent daily use at a high-EPA dose, and the omega-3 index in your cell membranes keeps rising for months after that. The [Cochrane review by Appleton and colleagues (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD004692.pub5) and the meta-analyses by [Liao and colleagues (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41398-019-0515-5) and [Sublette and colleagues (2011)](https://doi.org/10.4088/JCP.10r06634) all point the same way on dose composition: EPA content and consistency matter more than brand. See the [NIH ODS omega-3 fact sheet](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-HealthProfessional/) and the [EPA versus DHA post](/blog/epa-vs-dha-for-mood/).

## The two that run on both clocks

5-HTP acts on two clocks at once. A single dose peaks in the blood within a few hours, and some people notice a calming or sleepy effect the same day. A real change in mood builds over weeks, with the trials that reported a benefit generally assessing at around two to six weeks of daily use, which is roughly how antidepressants are timed ([2002 Cochrane review by Shaw, Turner and Del Mar](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD003198); review by [Turner et al., 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pharmthera.2005.06.004); comparison by [Jangid et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajp.2012.09.014)). Because 5-HTP raises serotonin it must never be combined with SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs, triptans, tramadol, dextromethorphan or St John's wort, and that rule does not relax with time on it. See [5-HTP and serotonin safety](/blog/5-htp-serotonin-safety/).

L-tryptophan behaves the same way. Taken at bedtime in a higher dose it can help some people fall asleep a little faster the same night, often within one to two hours ([Silber & Schmitt, 2010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2009.08.005); [Lindseth et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25858202/)). Used for mood it works far more slowly, with most people needing one to three weeks of steady daily use ([Shaw et al., 2002](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD003198)). It is a precursor the body converts rather than a drug acting directly, so it nudges the system over time.

## When to stop waiting

The more actionable question is how long before you can fairly give up, and those windows are in the table above. They are worth holding to, because without one you end up paying for something indefinitely on the hope that month four turns out different from month three.

Two things reset the clock rather than extending it. Changing the dose to one inside the studied range, since a product under-dosed against the trials was never running a fair test. And changing to a standardised or named-strain form, because the timelines on this page come from trials of specific extracts, not from generic powder. Changing brands at the same dose and form just runs the same trial over again.

Each product page lists the studied dose and the form the trials used: [L-theanine](/products/l-theanine/), [magnesium glycinate](/products/magnesium-glycinate/), [ashwagandha](/products/ashwagandha/), [rhodiola rosea](/products/rhodiola-rosea/), [saffron extract](/products/saffron-extract/), [omega-3 fish oil](/products/omega-3-fish-oil/), [vitamin D3](/products/vitamin-d3/), [the gut-brain probiotic](/products/probiotic-gut-brain/) and [5-HTP](/products/5-htp/). The [supplement side effects page](/blog/supplement-side-effects-what-to-expect/) covers what to do if something arrives sooner than the benefit does, and the [daily-use safety page](/blog/supplements-safe-to-take-every-day/) covers how long these are studied for in total, which is the natural next question once you know how long they take to start.

None of these supplements is a treatment for a diagnosed condition. If low mood, anxiety or insomnia has lasted weeks, a clinician is the right next step rather than a longer trial of a supplement. See [get help](/get-help/).
