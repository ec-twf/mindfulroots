Most people looking up a supplement's side effects want to answer one question: is what I am feeling a reason to stop taking it? For these seven, that depends less on which supplement you took than on which kind of effect you are dealing with, and the three kinds call for three different responses.

This is a your-money-or-your-life topic, so the cautions below are deliberate rather than decorative, and every serious signal is named with its source.

## The three kinds of side effect

The first kind is dose-dependent gut upset: nausea, cramping and loose stools. These scale with how much you took, they are the most common complaint for magnesium, 5-HTP and saffron, and they usually resolve if you lower the dose, split it across the day, or take it with food. They are a dosing signal rather than a sign of harm, and they say nothing about whether the supplement is working.

The second kind follows from what the supplement does. Ashwagandha tends to sedate and rhodiola tends to stimulate, so morning drowsiness from one and broken sleep from the other are really the same problem: a dose taken in the wrong half of the day. Moving the dose usually fixes that, where lowering it would only blunt the effect you wanted.

The third kind is short and different: rare liver injury with ashwagandha, serotonin syndrome when 5-HTP meets a serotonergic drug, and infection risk when a live probiotic meets a compromised immune system. These do not scale predictably with dose and they do not announce themselves gradually, so there is nothing useful to adjust. If one of them appears, stop and get medical advice.

## Magnesium glycinate

The usual effect is loose stools or mild stomach upset, and it is dose-related. The mechanism explains who gets it: magnesium the gut does not absorb draws water into the bowel. Glycinate is elemental magnesium bound to two molecules of glycine, and that chelated structure absorbs better than oxide, so less reaches the colon unabsorbed. This is also why oxide and citrate are sold as laxatives. A comparison of magnesium salts found chelated and organic forms more bioavailable than oxide ([Blancquaert et al., *Nutrients*, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28846654/)).

The number to hold is **350 mg of elemental magnesium per day from supplements**, the tolerable upper intake level for adults. It applies to supplements only, not food magnesium, and it was set to prevent diarrhoea rather than because crossing it is immediately toxic. The [NIH Office of Dietary Supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Magnesium-HealthProfessional/) confirms gut upset is the most frequent effect and clusters at higher intakes. Watch the word *elemental* on a label: a "500 mg" capsule often delivers far less.

The caution here has nothing to do with comfort. Chronic kidney disease or reduced kidney function changes the picture, because healthy kidneys clear surplus magnesium and impaired ones cannot. Accumulation can cause dangerously low blood pressure, muscle weakness, confusion and heart rhythm disturbance. Magnesium also reduces absorption of tetracycline and quinolone antibiotics and bisphosphonates taken at the same time, and some diuretics and proton-pump inhibitors shift magnesium levels. Spacing doses handles most of it; a pharmacist should confirm. See the [magnesium glycinate page](/products/magnesium-glycinate/).

## L-theanine

For most people there is nothing to report, which makes this the easiest of the seven. When effects appear they are mild and infrequent: a slight headache, lightheadedness, occasional drowsiness, mostly above the studied 100 to 200 mg. In the four-week randomised trial by [Hidese and colleagues (2019)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31623400/), 30 healthy adults took 200 mg daily and adverse events did not differ from placebo. A review by [Türközü and Şanlıer (2017)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26192072/) describes effects as uncommon and dose-related rather than a consistent pattern, and the [Memorial Sloan Kettering monograph](https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/l-theanine) lists no serious toxicity at supplemental doses.

The structural reason is that L-theanine is a water-soluble amino acid routinely consumed in tea, handled much as the body handles dietary amino acids, and holding GRAS status in the United States.

One interaction is worth raising with a prescriber. Part of how L-theanine eases the physical side of stress is a mild dampening of the stress-driven blood-pressure rise. In a healthy person that is small and harmless, but it could add to blood-pressure medication and leave you dizzy, which makes it a conversation rather than a reason to avoid theanine. There is no evidence that L-theanine is habit-forming, and no documented withdrawal. See the [L-theanine page](/products/l-theanine/).

## Probiotics

Expect gas, bloating, mild cramping and looser stools in the first days to weeks. The cause is the adjustment itself: introduced strains ferment some of the fibre and sugars passing through, producing gas, while the existing community shifts. The [NIH ODS probiotics fact sheet](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Probiotics-HealthProfessional/) notes these mild temporary symptoms are the most common effect in healthy people and that serious effects are rare. Large reviews of probiotics for antibiotic-associated diarrhoea found them well tolerated with side effects no more common than placebo ([Hempel et al., *JAMA*, 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22570464/)). Starting lower and building up eases the first week.

One smaller line of research is worth knowing. A 2018 study described a subset of people with significant bloating, gas and "brain fogginess" that the authors linked to small-intestinal bacterial overgrowth and D-lactic acid accumulation, in some cases associated with probiotic use ([Rao et al., *Clin Transl Gastroenterol*, 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29915215/)). It was a small study and not proof that probiotics commonly cause cognitive symptoms, but it is a fair reminder that a higher CFU count is not automatically a better one.

The serious signal is different in kind. These are live organisms, and in people whose defences are down they can rarely cause infection. Reported cases of bacteraemia and fungaemia concentrate almost entirely in specific groups: severely immunocompromised people, those critically ill in intensive care, people with central venous catheters, those recovering from major abdominal surgery, and premature infants. For these groups probiotics belong under direct medical supervision. Fever or severe abdominal pain is not a normal adjustment. Antibiotics kill the organisms, so separate the two by a couple of hours and continue through the course. See the [gut-brain probiotic page](/products/probiotic-gut-brain/).

## Saffron extract

At the roughly 30 mg a day used in mood trials the profile is mild: nausea, appetite change up or down, headache, sometimes drowsiness or dry mouth. A dedicated safety evaluation by [Modaghegh and colleagues (2008)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18693099/) gave healthy volunteers saffron tablets and found blood counts and vital signs within normal ranges at moderate doses. A meta-analysis by [Hausenblas and colleagues (2013)](https://doi.org/10.3736/jintegrmed2013056) and a review by [Lopresti and Drummond (2014)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24299602/) both reported no worrying safety signal at the doses tested, while noting long-term human data remain limited.

Saffron is the clearest case of dose-dependence in the group. Intakes well above the researched range have been associated with vomiting, dizziness, numbness and bleeding, and very high intakes are considered toxic. Since there is no evidence that exceeding about 30 mg a day buys more benefit, the higher-dose risk comes with no upside.

Several groups should skip saffron. Anyone pregnant, because saffron can stimulate the uterus at higher amounts, with caution while breastfeeding. Anyone on an SSRI, SNRI or other serotonergic drug, since saffron appears to influence serotonin. People with a bleeding disorder or on anticoagulant or antiplatelet medication, and stop before scheduled surgery. People with bipolar disorder. Saffron is also among the most adulterated ingredients on the market, often padded with cheaper plant material or dye, which makes third-party testing the main quality signal rather than a nice-to-have. See the [saffron extract page](/products/saffron-extract/).

## Ashwagandha

The common effects are drowsiness or a sedated, heavy feeling, plus nausea, loose stools and stomach discomfort, more likely at higher doses and often better with food. A safety and effectiveness review by [Tandon and Yadav (2020)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32305638/) found adverse effects across controlled trials mostly mild and transient, with drowsiness and digestive complaints reported most. The [NCCIH overview](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ashwagandha) agrees it is usually well tolerated short term while noting longer-term safety is less certain. Because sedation dominates, evening dosing is the standard fix.

Ashwagandha gets more space here than the others because of what follows. A case series by [Björnsson and colleagues (2020)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31766537/) described previously healthy people who developed liver injury with jaundice and raised liver enzymes after taking ashwagandha, all recovering after stopping. The NIH [LiverTox database](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK548536/) now lists it as an established, if uncommon, cause of drug-induced liver injury. **Stop and seek care for yellowing skin or eyes, dark urine, severe tiredness, or pain under the right ribs.** Second, ashwagandha can raise T3 and T4, which may push someone with an overactive or medicated thyroid out of range. Third, it can activate immune pathways, which is unwise in autoimmune disease.

The list of people who should avoid ashwagandha is longer than for the rest. Anyone pregnant, given the miscarriage concern, with caution while breastfeeding. People with thyroid conditions or on thyroid medication. People with autoimmune conditions such as lupus, rheumatoid arthritis or Hashimoto's. People with liver disease or already taking medicines that stress the liver. Stop before surgery, because of the sedative effect. It may add to sedatives, thyroid drugs and immunosuppressants. See the [ashwagandha page](/products/ashwagandha/).

## Rhodiola rosea

Rhodiola is a mild stimulant as well as an adaptogen, so its profile leans toward overstimulation rather than sedation. The reported effects are feeling wired or jittery, dizziness, dry mouth, and disturbed sleep when taken late, sometimes irritability or vivid dreams.

| Effect | How common | What helps |
|---|---|---|
| Jittery, overstimulated | Most reported | Lower dose, take in the morning |
| Disturbed sleep | Common if taken late | Take before noon |
| Dizziness | Occasional | Lower dose, take with food |
| Dry mouth | Occasional | Usually settles |
| Irritability, vivid dreams | Less common | Reduce dose or stop |

The [NCCIH](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/rhodiola) describes rhodiola as generally well tolerated in short-term use with effects such as dizziness and dry mouth, while noting long-term safety is not well established. A systematic review by [Ishaque and colleagues (2012)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22643043/) found adverse events generally mild and infrequent but cautioned that many trials carried a high risk of bias, so the reassurance rests on imperfect data. The tolerability record attaches to standardised extracts at the studied 200 to 400 mg, often around 3% rosavins and 1% salidroside, not to unmeasured crude root.

Two adjustments resolve most problems: take it earlier, and start at the low end. Stacking it with a lot of caffeine amplifies the wired feeling.

A few groups should skip rhodiola. People with bipolar disorder, since stimulating supplements can provoke agitation or a manic swing. Anyone pregnant or breastfeeding, for lack of data. Anyone on an antidepressant, stimulant, blood-pressure or diabetes medication should check first. Pause before surgery, and stop for a fast or irregular heartbeat or significant agitation. Rhodiola is also frequently adulterated or substituted with cheaper look-alike species, so a real certificate of analysis matters. See the [rhodiola rosea page](/products/rhodiola-rosea/).

## 5-HTP

The everyday effects are digestive and dose-tracking: nausea is the standout, with stomach cramps, appetite loss and diarrhoea, often easier at a lower dose or with food. Drowsiness is the other common one, which is part of why evening dosing is popular. A review by [Turner and colleagues (2006)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16023217/) describes this gastrointestinal pattern as the main tolerability limit at typical doses, and [Examine's 5-HTP page](https://examine.com/supplements/5-htp/) reaches the same practical conclusion: start low, around 50 mg.

The rule here is absolute. Because 5-HTP raises serotonin, combining it with anything else that raises serotonin can push levels too high and trigger serotonin syndrome. That includes SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs, tricyclics, triptans, tramadol, dextromethorphan and St John's wort. Described in detail by [Boyer and Shannon (2005)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15784664/), it ranges from agitation, a fast heartbeat, sweating, shivering and muscle twitching in milder cases to high fever and confusion in severe ones, and it can escalate quickly. If you take a serotonergic medication and notice several of these after adding 5-HTP, seek urgent care and name every product you take.

There is also a specific purity history. In 1989 an outbreak of eosinophilia-myalgia syndrome was linked to contaminated batches of the related supplement L-tryptophan, and a contaminant nicknamed "peak X" was later detected in some 5-HTP samples. A safety review by [Das and colleagues (2004)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14732446/) concluded that pharmaceutical-grade 5-HTP appears safe while flagging purity as the thing that matters. That is the strongest practical argument for third-party testing here.

Anyone on a serotonergic medication should not take 5-HTP, and that is the one with no exceptions. Beyond it, skip it if you are pregnant or breastfeeding. Anyone scheduled for surgery, since some anaesthetic agents are serotonergic, so stop well before and tell the team. People with liver or kidney conditions, and children, only under clinical guidance. Avoid "mood" and "sleep" blends that stack 5-HTP with other serotonergic ingredients, because each addition compounds the risk and makes a reaction harder to trace. ## What to look for if you are prone to side effects

Three label details matter more than the brand.

**A single-ingredient product.** It means one effect traces to one cause, and you can stop one thing without losing the other. Blends stack actives you cannot dose separately, which is the opposite of what you want while you are still establishing tolerance.

**A dose per serving that matches the trials**, printed clearly, not a bigger number. For magnesium that means elemental magnesium stated per serving so you can stay under 350 mg. For the standardised extracts it means the marker compound and percentage: withanolides for ashwagandha, rosavins and salidroside for rhodiola, crocins and safranal for saffron. For probiotics it means named strains with a strain code, such as *Lactobacillus rhamnosus* GG, and CFU guaranteed through end of shelf life rather than at manufacture. For 5-HTP it means a product that lets you start at 50 mg.

**Third-party testing** for identity, potency and contaminants. It matters most for the ingredients with a documented adulteration or contamination history, which here means saffron, rhodiola and 5-HTP.

Then start at the low end, take it with food, and change one thing at a time. ## When a side effect needs a clinician

Across all seven, the same situations turn a personal judgement into a conversation: pregnancy or breastfeeding, any prescription medication, kidney or liver disease, thyroid conditions, autoimmune conditions, a suppressed immune system, bipolar disorder or another diagnosed mood condition, and upcoming surgery.

None of these supplements is a treatment for a diagnosed condition, and none is a substitute for care. If you take regular medication and are unsure whether it interacts, ask your prescriber or pharmacist and name every product you take.
