Saffron (*Crocus sativus*) is the deep-red stigma of the saffron crocus, taken as a standardised extract to support mood and emotional balance. For premenstrual mood swings and irritability, the evidence points to a specific, modest dose rather than a vague "take some saffron." A daily 30 mg of standardised extract is the amount that eased premenstrual symptoms in the main clinical trial, taken steadily across the cycle. ## Why saffron is studied for premenstrual mood

The premenstrual phase, the roughly one to two weeks before a period, is when many people notice a dip in mood, more irritability, and a shorter fuse. Saffron is of interest here because its active compounds, safranal and crocin, are associated with serotonergic activity, the same broad pathway that mood-related medications act on. A double-blind, placebo-controlled trial by [Agha-Hosseini and colleagues (2008)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18271889/) gave women with PMS 30 mg of saffron a day over two menstrual cycles and reported significantly greater improvement in premenstrual symptoms and in depression scores than placebo. That study is the anchor for the 30 mg figure.

The wider mood literature backs the plausibility. A meta-analysis by [Hausenblas and colleagues (2013)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24299602/) found saffron outperformed placebo for depressive symptoms across several trials, and a systematic review by [Lopresti and Drummond (2014)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25384672/) reached a similar conclusion while noting most studies were small and short. The tier here is emerging-to-moderate: promising and consistent, not yet definitive.

## The dose and how to time it

The practical takeaway is narrow, which is a good thing. Use 30 mg of standardised saffron extract per day, commonly split into 15 mg in the morning and 15 mg in the evening. In the trials this was taken every day, continuously, rather than only during the premenstrual window, and mood effects emerged over weeks rather than within a single cycle. Judge it over at least two full cycles at a steady dose.

More is not better with saffron. The clinical benefit sits at this low dose, higher intakes raise the chance of side effects such as nausea or headache, and amounts far above supplement levels are toxic. There is no reason to exceed 30 mg a day.

## What to look for in a saffron extract for PMS mood support

Because the benefit is tied to a specific dose and to standardised actives, the label is where a saffron purchase succeeds or fails. Choose a product that names a standardised extract and states its key actives, usually a set percentage of safranal or crocin, and that delivers 30 mg per daily serving to match the studied amount. A branded, clinically trialled extract such as affron is one way to know you are getting what the research used. Confirm third-party testing (a real concern given saffron is among the most adulterated botanicals on the market) and avoid proprietary blends that hide the saffron dose behind a combined milligram total. Skip products padded with unrelated extracts if your goal is simply premenstrual mood support. ## Cautions and interactions

Saffron supports mood; it does not treat premenstrual dysphoric disorder, depression, or any diagnosed condition. At 30 mg a day it was well tolerated in trials, but a few points matter. Avoid saffron in pregnancy, since higher doses have been linked to uterine stimulation. If you take an antidepressant, saffron acts on overlapping serotonin pathways, so combine the two only under medical guidance. It may also add to the effect of blood-thinning medication. Severe, disabling premenstrual mood symptoms deserve a proper clinical assessment rather than a supplement alone. Talk to your doctor or pharmacist before starting saffron, especially if you take any regular medication.
