affron and Satiereal are both branded saffron extracts made from the same plant, so the real question is not which is stronger but which was built for your goal. affron is the one trialled for mood at 28 mg a day. Satiereal is the one trialled for curbing snacking and appetite. Because they are standardised and dosed for different endpoints, their milligram numbers do not compare, and picking by the bigger number is the common mistake.

If mood support is why you are here, the saffron your evidence points to is affron. Our [saffron extract page](/products/saffron-extract/) and the [low mood guide](/guides/low-mood/) cover where the ingredient fits before brand names enter the picture.

## Same saffron, two different jobs

Saffron is the dried stigma of the *Crocus sativus* flower, used for centuries as a spice and, more recently, studied as a mood support. Its active compounds include crocins, safranal and picrocrocin. A branded extract standardises some of those markers so that every capsule delivers a known amount, which is what lets a manufacturer run trials and lets you repeat the studied dose.

affron and Satiereal both start from that same stigma. Where they part ways is the endpoint each was developed and standardised for. affron is standardised to a set level of its marker compounds and was trialled at 28 mg a day specifically for mood. Satiereal was developed around appetite and snacking behaviour and studied at a higher daily dose for that goal. Same plant, two different research programmes, two different reasons to reach for it.

## What the trials measured

The affron mood research ran in healthy adults reporting low mood and in adolescents, using 28 mg a day over several weeks and tracking mood and stress scales. Satiereal's published work sat in a different field entirely: a randomised study in mildly overweight women measured snacking frequency and feelings of fullness, not mood. That split matters for a buyer, because an extract's evidence only supports the outcome it was tested against. Choosing Satiereal for mood means paying for research that was never about mood.

Saffron as a whole has a moderate but growing evidence base for mood support, summarised in independent reviews such as [Memorial Sloan Kettering's herb monograph](https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/saffron) and [Examine's saffron page](https://examine.com/supplements/saffron/). Read the brand claims against that wider picture rather than against each other.

## What to look for in a saffron extract supplement

Once you know your goal, the label does the rest of the work. For mood, look for affron named specifically, a dose that reaches the studied 28 mg a day, and a clear statement of the plant part and standardisation marker. For appetite, look for Satiereal at its studied daily dose. Either way, avoid a product that only says "saffron extract" with no marker compound and no dose, because you cannot line it up against any trial. Prioritise third-party testing, since saffron is a high-value spice that is sometimes adulterated with cheaper plant material. Our [saffron extract page](/products/saffron-extract/) sets out the evidence and typical use so you can match a product to what was actually studied, and the [low mood guide](/guides/low-mood/) shows where saffron sits beside other mood supports. A named brand, a studied dose and independent testing tell you far more than a "high strength" flash on the front.

## When the premium is not worth it

If your goal is general mood support and you find a plain saffron extract that clearly states its stigma origin, its standardisation marker and a 28 mg dose, it can be a reasonable choice at a lower price. The premium you pay for affron buys a dose that lines up with named trials, which matters most when you want to reproduce a study's conditions. It buys less when a transparent generic already gives you the plant part, the marker and the dose. What is never worth paying for is a bigger Satiereal number in the belief it will help mood more, because that milligram figure belongs to appetite research, not mood.

## Cautions

Saffron is generally well tolerated at these low doses, but it is not for everyone. High doses well above supplement levels can be toxic, and saffron may add to the effect of medicines that influence mood or blood clotting. Avoid it in pregnancy, where higher amounts have been linked to uterine stimulation. If you take an antidepressant or any prescription medication, check with your prescriber or pharmacist before starting, whichever extract you choose. Saffron supports mood; it does not treat depression or any diagnosed condition.
