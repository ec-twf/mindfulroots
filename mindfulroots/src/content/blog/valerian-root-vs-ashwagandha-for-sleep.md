Valerian (*Valeriana officinalis*) is a root taken as a fast-acting, mildly sedative sleep aid, while ashwagandha (*Withania somnifera*) is an adaptogenic root taken over weeks to support the body's stress response. Both end up in the sleep aisle, but they get there by different routes and on different timelines. Valerian is something you take near bedtime for a same-night effect; ashwagandha is a daily supplement that works on the stress and cortisol pattern underneath poor sleep. The right pick depends less on which is "stronger" and more on why you are lying awake. For occasional trouble dropping off, valerian fits; for sleeplessness tied to ongoing stress, ashwagandha is the better match.

If your sleep problem is stress-driven, our [ashwagandha page](/products/ashwagandha/) covers the standardised extracts and how they are dosed. This post compares the two head to head so you can tell which one your kind of sleeplessness calls for.

## The quick verdict

Choose **valerian** if you occasionally struggle to fall asleep and want something that acts the same night, taken around bedtime. Choose **ashwagandha** if your sleeplessness rides on background stress or a raised-cortisol pattern, and you are willing to take it daily for a few weeks to feel the benefit. They work on different timescales, so this is genuinely a "depends on your pattern" answer rather than a single winner, and some people use valerian as needed while ashwagandha does the slower background work.

## Valerian: the fast bedtime sedative

Valerian has been used as a sleep herb for centuries, and it behaves like a gentle sedative rather than an adaptogen. It is thought to work by nudging GABA activity, the same calming system that many sleep drugs target, which fits its near-bedtime, same-night use. The catch is that the trial evidence is mixed. A [2006 systematic review and meta-analysis by Bent and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17145239/) concluded that valerian might improve sleep quality but that the studies were inconsistent and often low quality, so the effect is real for some people and modest at best on average.

The US [NIH Office of Dietary Supplements valerian fact sheet](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Valerian-HealthProfessional/) reaches a similar verdict: valerian is generally well tolerated short-term, but the evidence that it reliably helps sleep is limited. That places it at an emerging evidence tier. Its practical appeal is speed and as-needed use, not a strong or guaranteed effect.

## Ashwagandha: the slow stress route to sleep

Ashwagandha does not sedate you at bedtime. It works on the stress axis over weeks, and better sleep tends to follow as stress and cortisol settle. A [2019 randomized trial by Langade and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31728244/) found that a standardised root extract improved sleep quality and anxiety measures over eight weeks in adults with insomnia, and the herb's broader stress data support the same mechanism. Memorial Sloan Kettering's [About Herbs page on ashwagandha](https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/ashwagandha) summarises reasonable short-term stress evidence while noting interaction data is still limited.

This makes ashwagandha the better fit when the thing keeping you up is worry and a wired, tired feeling rather than a one-off restless night. It asks for patience: daily use, and several weeks before you judge it. It also carries considerations valerian does not, including effects on thyroid hormones and rare liver reactions, which is why people on medication should check first.

## Valerian or ashwagandha: which supplement to buy for sleep

Pick the herb that matches your pattern, then choose on quality. For **valerian**, favour a **standardised extract** with a stated amount, taken 30 minutes to two hours before bed, and use it as-needed rather than indefinitely. For **ashwagandha**, favour a **standardised root extract** such as KSM-66 or Sensoril with a stated withanolide percentage, at a **studied daily dose** (commonly 300 mg twice daily), and give it several weeks. For either, choose **third-party tested** products for label accuracy and avoid "sleep blends" that hide each herb's dose among melatonin and other actives, which makes side effects hard to trace. Do not combine either with alcohol or sedatives without advice. ## Cautions

Valerian can cause morning grogginess, headache, or vivid dreams, and it should not be mixed with alcohol, sedatives, or before driving until you know how it affects you; long-term safety is not well established. Ashwagandha can raise thyroid hormone levels, rarely affects the liver, and may stimulate immune activity, so people on thyroid medication, with autoimmune conditions, or who are pregnant should avoid it or check with a clinician first. Neither herb treats an insomnia or anxiety disorder; they offer support at most. If sleeplessness or stress is affecting your daily life, talk to a clinician rather than self-treating indefinitely.
