Rhodiola rosea is an adaptogenic herb, traditionally grown in cold mountain regions, used as a supplement to support energy, mental stamina, and resilience under stress. The practical question most people arrive with is a dosing one, so here is the short version: the studied range is 200 to 400 mg of a standardised extract per day, taken in the morning, with cycling optional rather than essential. You can read more on the [rhodiola rosea page](/products/rhodiola-rosea/), and the [energy guide](/guides/energy/) shows where it fits among other supports.

## What the trials actually dosed

The evidence base is built on standardised extracts, not raw powder, and the standardisation is what makes a dose comparable across studies. Most research uses extracts standardised to roughly 3% rosavins and 1% salidroside, the profile of the SHR-5 extract that appears throughout the literature. An early placebo-controlled study by [Darbinyan and colleagues (2000)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11081987/) gave physicians on night duty 170 mg a day and measured less mental fatigue. Later work by [Olsson and colleagues (2009)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19016404/) used 576 mg daily of SHR-5 for burnout-related fatigue, and a trial by [Cropley and colleagues (2015)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25837277/) used 400 mg daily for symptoms of stress. The through-line is a daily dose in the low hundreds of milligrams, not the gram-scale doses seen with some other supplements.

## How to time it

Rhodiola is mildly activating for many people, so timing follows its effect rather than a strict clock. A single morning dose on an empty stomach suits most users. If you prefer to split it, take the second portion before mid-afternoon so it does not sit on top of your bedtime. Taking it with a heavy meal can blunt the effect a little, which is why an empty stomach is the common recommendation. The independent review at [Examine](https://examine.com/supplements/rhodiola-rosea/) reaches the same practical range.

## Do you need to cycle it?

Cycling means taking planned breaks, such as five days on and two off, or a week off every month or two. It is a common herbalist practice, but it is not well tested. Most controlled trials simply dosed daily for four to twelve weeks and reported an acceptable safety profile without a washout, and the [NIH Office of Dietary Supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/list-all/) does not set a rhodiola-specific limit. The honest position: cycling is reasonable if you want periodic checks on whether it still helps, but there is no evidence you must cycle it to keep it safe.

## What to look for in a standardised rhodiola rosea supplement

The single most useful thing on a rhodiola label is the standardisation. Look for an extract that names its rosavin and salidroside percentages, ideally around 3% rosavins and 1% salidroside, or that specifies the SHR-5 extract used in trials. Confirm the milligrams of extract per capsule so you can hit the 200 to 400 mg studied range without guesswork. Third-party testing (NSF, USP, or Informed Choice) matters here because rhodiola is sometimes adulterated with cheaper related species. Favour a single-ingredient product over a proprietary "energy blend," where the actual rhodiola dose is often hidden. ## Cautions

Rhodiola is generally well tolerated, but it is not for everyone. Because it can be stimulating, some people report jitteriness, irritability, or trouble sleeping, especially at higher doses or late in the day. It has been used for people with bipolar spectrum conditions only under supervision, since activating supplements can be a problem there. If you take prescription medication, particularly antidepressants or blood-pressure drugs, check with your prescriber first.
