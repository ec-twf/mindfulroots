5-HTP (5-hydroxytryptophan) is a compound the body makes from the amino acid tryptophan and uses to produce serotonin; as a supplement, usually extracted from *Griffonia simplicifolia* seeds, it is taken to support mood and sleep. Because it feeds directly into serotonin production, its side-effect profile is dominated by one serious concern, serotonin syndrome, alongside milder digestive and drowsiness effects. This is a your-money-or-your-life topic, so the cautions here are deliberate. The [5-HTP page](/products/5-htp/) covers the product details, and the [low mood guide](/guides/low-mood/) sets it among other options.

## The most common side effects

The effects most people notice are digestive. Nausea is the standout, along with stomach cramps, loss of appetite, and diarrhoea, and these tend to track with dose, so they are more likely at higher amounts and often ease when the dose drops or the capsule is taken with food. Drowsiness is the other common one, which is part of why evening dosing is popular. A review of 5-HTP by [Turner and colleagues (2006)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16023217/) describes this gastrointestinal pattern as the main tolerability limit at typical doses. The reference overview on [Examine's 5-HTP page](https://examine.com/supplements/5-htp/) reaches the same practical conclusion: start low to manage nausea.

## The serious risk: serotonin syndrome

This is the side effect that matters most. Because 5-HTP increases serotonin, combining it with anything else that raises serotonin can push levels too high and trigger serotonin syndrome. That includes SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs, tricyclic antidepressants, triptans for migraine, tramadol, and St John's wort. Serotonin syndrome, described in detail by [Boyer and Shannon (2005)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15784664/), ranges from agitation, a fast heartbeat, sweating, shivering, and muscle twitching in milder cases to high fever and confusion in severe ones. If you take a serotonergic medication and notice several of these after adding 5-HTP, seek urgent medical care and name every product you take. The safe rule is simple: do not combine 5-HTP with antidepressants or other serotonergic drugs without a doctor's supervision.

## The contamination history

5-HTP carries a specific historical footnote worth knowing. In 1989 an outbreak of eosinophilia-myalgia syndrome (EMS), a serious condition, was linked to contaminated batches of the related supplement L-tryptophan, and a contaminant nicknamed "peak X" was later detected in some 5-HTP samples too. A safety review by [Das and colleagues (2004)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14732446/) examined this and concluded that pharmaceutical-grade 5-HTP appears safe, while flagging the importance of product purity. That is the strongest practical argument for buying only third-party-tested 5-HTP.

## What to look for in a low-dose 5-HTP supplement

If a clinician agrees 5-HTP is appropriate for you, the label choices matter. Look for a single-ingredient product that lets you start low, around 50 mg, with the dose per capsule stated clearly so you can find the smallest effective amount and keep nausea manageable. Given the contamination history, third-party testing (NSF, USP, or Informed Choice) is not optional here; it is the main quality signal. Avoid "mood" or "sleep" blends that stack 5-HTP with other serotonergic ingredients such as St John's wort or tryptophan, because each addition compounds the serotonin risk and makes any reaction harder to trace. ## Who should skip 5-HTP

Some people should not take 5-HTP at all, or only with medical oversight:

- Anyone on an SSRI, SNRI, MAOI, tricyclic, triptan, tramadol, or St John's wort, because of serotonin syndrome risk.
- People who are pregnant or breastfeeding, where safety is not established.
- Anyone scheduled for surgery, since some anaesthetic agents are serotonergic; stop well before and tell your team.
- People with liver or kidney conditions, and children, unless a clinician is guiding use.

If you are unsure whether a medication you take is serotonergic, treat 5-HTP as if it interacts and ask your prescriber or pharmacist first. It is not a treatment for depression or any diagnosed condition, and it is not a substitute for care.
