Take ashwagandha with a meal. It is not a strict rule, but it is the more comfortable choice: the side effect people report most often is mild stomach upset, and that tends to appear when the supplement is taken on an empty stomach. Ashwagandha's active compounds, called withanolides, are fat-soluble, so a meal with a little fat is a sensible match. Most of the clinical trials dosed it with or just after food, which makes copying that habit an easy default.

## Why food helps

Ashwagandha (*Withania somnifera*) is an adaptogen used to support the body's response to stress. Its effect is not immediate the way a painkiller is; it builds over weeks of daily use, so the practical questions are about tolerability and routine rather than about a single perfectly timed dose. Taking it with food answers both. A meal buffers the stomach, which reduces the nausea, loose stools, or queasiness some people notice, a point the [NCCIH overview of ashwagandha](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ashwagandha) lists among its common mild effects.

There is also an absorption logic. Withanolides, the steroidal lactones thought to drive ashwagandha's activity, are fat-soluble. Fat-soluble compounds are generally absorbed more readily in the presence of dietary fat, which is why pairing the dose with a normal meal, ideally one containing some fat, is a reasonable habit rather than a gimmick. The [Memorial Sloan Kettering herb entry on ashwagandha](https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/ashwagandha) similarly frames it as a well-tolerated supplement best taken as part of a steady daily routine.

## What the trials actually did

The human research is a useful guide here, because it shows how ashwagandha was taken in the studies that reported benefits. A randomised, placebo-controlled trial by [Chandrasekhar and colleagues (2012)](https://doi.org/10.4103/0253-7176.106022) gave adults a standardised root extract twice daily and reported lower stress and cortisol over 60 days. A later trial by [Salve and colleagues (2019)](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.6466) used a similar twice-daily schedule and found improvements in stress and sleep markers. A study by [Lopresti and colleagues (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1097/MD.0000000000017186) reported better sleep and stress scores with a standardised extract taken daily.

| Study | Schedule | What it measured |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chandrasekhar 2012 | Twice daily, 60 days | Stress and cortisol |
| Salve 2019 | Twice daily, 8 weeks | Stress, sleep, cortisol |
| Lopresti 2019 | Daily, 8 weeks | Sleep and stress scores |

The common thread is a divided daily dose taken over weeks, generally around meals. None of these trials found that food stopped it working, which is the reassurance most people are looking for.

## What to look for in an ashwagandha supplement to take with food

The label decides how well this works in practice. Choose a standardised root extract and check the withanolide percentage, since that is the marker the trials tracked; the two most researched branded extracts are KSM-66 and Sensoril, and both state their standardisation. Match the dose to the research, which usually falls between 300 and 600 mg per day of extract, often split into two. A capsule or tablet is the easiest form to take with a meal and to keep consistent. Look for third-party testing so the withanolide content on the label reflects what is in the bottle, and avoid "proprietary blends" that hide the actual extract dose behind a mix of fillers.

## Cautions

Ashwagandha is well tolerated by most healthy adults, but it is not for everyone. People who are pregnant or breastfeeding should avoid it. Because it can nudge thyroid hormone levels and may lower blood sugar and blood pressure, anyone with a thyroid condition, diabetes, or who takes sedatives or immune-suppressing medicines should check with a clinician first. Rare reports of liver injury mean you should stop and seek advice if you notice unusual fatigue, dark urine, or yellowing skin.
