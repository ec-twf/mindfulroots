For anxiety that feels keyed-up, cortisol-driven, and tangled with poor sleep, ashwagandha is the better-evidenced starting point and the more directly calming of the two. For stress that shows up as burnout, flat energy, and mental fog rather than a racing pulse, rhodiola rosea is the more natural fit. They are both adaptogens, herbs taken to help the body cope with stress, but they pull in slightly different directions, and the honest verdict is that the right one depends on how your stress actually feels.

## How each one works

Rhodiola rosea is a cold-climate root standardised to compounds called rosavins and salidroside. Its reputation is as an anti-fatigue adaptogen: it is taken to sharpen a tired mind and lift stress-related exhaustion rather than to sedate. A trial by [Cropley and colleagues (2015)](https://doi.org/10.1002/ptr.5486) reported reductions in self-rated anxiety, stress, and fatigue over 14 days in people taking a standardised rhodiola extract, though it was an open-label study without a placebo arm, so it sits lower on the evidence ladder.

Ashwagandha (*Withania somnifera*) works more on the calming end. Its withanolide compounds are linked to lower cortisol, the main stress hormone. A randomised, placebo-controlled trial by [Chandrasekhar and colleagues (2012)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23439798/) found a high-concentration root extract reduced stress and anxiety scores and lowered serum cortisol over 60 days. A later controlled study by [Salve and colleagues (2019)](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.6466) reported similar cortisol and stress reductions. The [independent rhodiola summary at Examine](https://examine.com/supplements/rhodiola-rosea/) rates rhodiola's stress evidence as promising but thinner than ashwagandha's.

## Head-to-head

| | Rhodiola rosea | Ashwagandha |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main effect | Anti-fatigue, mildly activating | Calming, cortisol-lowering |
| Best for | Burnout, low drive, mental fog | Keyed-up anxiety, poor sleep |
| Onset | Often 1–2 weeks | Builds over 8–12 weeks |
| Typical study dose | ~200–400 mg standardised | ~300–600 mg root extract |
| Timing | Morning (can disturb sleep late) | Often evening |

The table makes the split clear. Neither is a false universal winner. If your anxiety is a wired, cannot-switch-off feeling, ashwagandha's calming, cortisol-focused profile is the more logical and better-supported choice. If your stress reads as depletion and burnout, rhodiola's activating profile fits better.

## What the evidence shows

For rhodiola, trials of the standardised SHR-5 extract reported reduced fatigue and improved mental performance in stressed or fatigued adults, including a study in physicians on night duty ([Olsson et al., 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19016404/)). A later trial found rhodiola comparable to a low-dose antidepressant for mild depressive symptoms with fewer side effects, though the effect size was modest ([Mao et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26502953/)).

For ashwagandha, the most-replicated protocol, 300 mg of root extract twice daily, reduced perceived stress and cortisol over eight weeks ([Chandrasekhar et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23439798/)), and the independent [NCCIH overview](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ashwagandha) treats these stress and sleep findings as promising but still developing.

Both bodies of evidence are best described as emerging to moderate: consistent directions, small to medium trials, limited long-term data. Neither herb treats a diagnosed anxiety disorder.

## Which to buy for anxiety

Start by matching the herb to your pattern rather than the marketing. For keyed-up, cortisol-driven anxiety and disturbed sleep, choose [ashwagandha](/products/ashwagandha/) and look for a standardised root extract, commonly listed as KSM-66 or a stated withanolide percentage, at a dose in the studied range. For stress that shows up as fatigue and low drive, choose [rhodiola rosea](/products/rhodiola-rosea/) standardised to about 3 percent rosavins and 1 percent salidroside, and take it earlier in the day. For either, confirm third-party testing and a clear per-capsule dose rather than a proprietary blend that hides the amounts. Our [anxiety guide](/guides/anxiety/) shows where each sits among other options. Adaptogens support stress resilience; they do not treat an anxiety disorder.

## Cautions

Both are generally well tolerated in the short term, but neither is a substitute for care where anxiety is severe or persistent. Ashwagandha is not advised in pregnancy and can interact with thyroid and sedative medication; rhodiola can feel over-activating and disturb sleep if taken late. If you take prescription medication or manage a diagnosed condition, talk to your prescriber before starting either one.
