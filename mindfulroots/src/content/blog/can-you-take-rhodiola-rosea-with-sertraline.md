Rhodiola rosea is an adaptogenic herb, a plant used to help the body cope with physical and mental stress, standardised to its active rosavins and salidroside. Can you take it with sertraline, one of the most prescribed SSRIs for depression and anxiety? There is no confirmed interaction on record, but this is not a green light. Rhodiola acts on the same serotonin machinery that sertraline works on, so combining them carries a theoretical serotonin-syndrome risk that a documented-cases search will not show you. This is a decision for your prescriber, not a supplement you should quietly add.

If you are weighing rhodiola for stress or low energy while on an antidepressant, our [rhodiola rosea hub](/products/rhodiola-rosea/) covers extract types and typical doses. This post stays on the sertraline question and why the caution is stronger than the thin evidence looks.

## Why the caution is real despite no reported cases

Sertraline is a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor. It works by blocking the transporter that clears serotonin from the synapse, so more serotonin stays available. Serotonin syndrome happens when levels climb too high, usually because two serotonergic agents stack. The classic culprits are SSRIs plus MAO inhibitors, triptans, tramadol, or St John's wort.

Rhodiola is not a drug, but laboratory work has flagged a relevant property. A [2009 study by van Diermen and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2009.01.032) found that rhodiola root extract inhibited monoamine oxidase A and B in vitro. MAO is the enzyme that breaks serotonin down. Inhibiting it is precisely how the most dangerous SSRI interactions occur. In-vitro activity does not always translate to a meaningful effect in a person swallowing a capsule, and no clinical case of rhodiola-plus-SSRI serotonin syndrome has been published. But absence of a report is not proof of safety, especially for a herb taken by far fewer people than prescription drugs and rarely tracked in interaction databases.

## What the evidence actually shows

There is no human trial of rhodiola combined with sertraline. What exists is evidence for rhodiola's own effects on stress and fatigue, plus the mechanistic signal above.

| Study | Design | What was tested | Key finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| Darbinyan 2000 | RCT, 56 physicians | Rhodiola vs placebo, night duty | Less mental fatigue |
| Olsson 2009 | RCT, 60 adults | SHR-5 for stress fatigue | Lower cortisol, less fatigue |
| van Diermen 2009 | In-vitro assay | MAO-A and MAO-B activity | Rhodiola inhibited both |
| Mao 2015 | RCT, 57 adults | Rhodiola vs sertraline for depression | Both improved; rhodiola milder, fewer side effects |

One trial is worth singling out. [Mao and colleagues (2015)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.phymed.2015.10.001) compared rhodiola against sertraline head-to-head in mild-to-moderate depression. Both reduced symptoms, and rhodiola produced fewer side effects, though sertraline had a slightly larger effect. That study compared the two as alternatives, not as a combination, and it does not tell you they are safe together. For rhodiola's stress and fatigue benefits, [Olsson (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-0028-1088346) and [Darbinyan (2000)](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0944-7113%2800%2980078-1) put it at an emerging-to-moderate tier. The US [National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/rhodiola) notes the human evidence is limited and calls for caution with medications.

## What to look for in a rhodiola rosea supplement while on an SSRI

The first thing to look for is your prescriber's agreement, before any product detail. If you have that, a few label markers separate a supplement worth trialling from a vague one. Choose an extract standardised to roughly **3 percent rosavins and 1 percent salidroside**, the ratio used in most trials, so the dose you take resembles the dose that was studied. Favour **third-party testing** for identity and contaminants, since adulteration is common in the adaptogen market. Start at the **low end, around 200 mg**, taken in the morning, so any stimulation or interaction shows up gradually rather than all at once. Avoid combination "stress blends" that hide the rhodiola dose among other serotonergic botanicals, which only muddies an already sensitive picture. ## Cautions and interactions

This is a your-money-or-your-life pairing, so err toward caution. The core concern is additive serotonin activity. Even with no reported cases, the mechanistic signal from MAO inhibition means the theoretical risk of serotonin syndrome is not zero. Learn the warning signs: agitation, restlessness, heavy sweating, shivering, muscle twitching, a racing heart, or confusion. If several appear after starting rhodiola, stop and get medical help.

Rhodiola can also feel stimulating and may cause jitteriness or trouble sleeping, which can blur with the activation some people feel early on sertraline. People with bipolar disorder should avoid rhodiola without supervision, since stimulating agents can trigger agitation or mania. Pregnancy, breastfeeding, and childhood sit outside rhodiola's tested range.

Thin evidence for this specific pairing is not evidence that it is safe for you. If you take sertraline or any other antidepressant, do not add rhodiola on your own. Talk to your prescriber or pharmacist first.
