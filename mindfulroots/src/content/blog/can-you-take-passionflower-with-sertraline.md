There is no well-documented dangerous interaction between passionflower and sertraline (Zoloft), but the pair has not been properly studied together, so this is a "check first" situation rather than a clear yes. The most realistic effect is a little extra drowsiness or calm, not a severe reaction. Because both act on the nervous system and the evidence is thin, the sensible step is to ask the doctor who prescribed your sertraline before you combine them.

If your aim is a calmer day while staying on your medication, there is a better-studied option worth raising with your prescriber, covered lower down. You can see what to check on the [L-theanine page](/products/l-theanine/), and the [anxiety guide](/guides/anxiety/) sets it among other supports.

## What passionflower does

Passionflower (*Passiflora incarnata*) is a traditional calming herb. Its relaxing effect is generally attributed to compounds that raise activity at GABA receptors, the brain's main calming pathway, rather than to any strong action on serotonin. An early double-blind trial by [Akhondzadeh and colleagues (2001)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11679026/) found passionflower comparable to a low dose of the anxiety drug oxazepam for generalised anxiety, though the study was small and short. Memorial Sloan Kettering's [About Herbs summary of passionflower](https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/passionflower) reaches a similar view: mild sedative promise, modest evidence, and a note that it can add to the effect of other sedatives.

Sertraline is a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor sold as Zoloft. It raises serotonin activity to support mood and anxiety, and a clinician prescribes and monitors it.

## The theoretical interaction

Two mechanisms are worth thinking about, and neither is a proven danger. The first is additive calm: passionflower can be mildly sedating, so on top of any drowsiness from sertraline, or alongside other sleep aids or alcohol, you might feel more tired or foggy than usual. The second is the serotonin question. Serotonin syndrome is a real risk when serotonergic agents are stacked, and [Boyer and Shannon (2005)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15784664/) describe how it develops. That risk is well established for combinations such as an SSRI with 5-HTP, L-tryptophan, or St John's wort. Passionflower is not a recognised high-serotonin agent, so it does not carry the same red flag. The honest limit is that this specific pairing has not been trialled, so "no reported problem" is not the same as "proven safe".

## A better-studied calming option: L-theanine

If you want something calming while staying on sertraline, L-theanine has more direct human evidence than passionflower. It is the amino acid found in green tea, and a randomised trial by [Hidese and colleagues (2019)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31623400/) reported lower stress-related scores in healthy adults taking 200 mg a day, with good tolerability. It has not been formally tested with sertraline either, so it remains a prescriber conversation, but its profile is reassuring and it is not a serotonin precursor.

## Cautions and the bottom line

Watch for extra drowsiness if you do use passionflower with sertraline, and stop it if you feel over-sedated. Treat any agitation, confusion, sweating, shivering, or a fast heartbeat as a reason to seek urgent medical advice, since those can signal a serotonin problem whatever the cause. None of this replaces individual guidance.

Do not start, stop, or combine anything with your antidepressant without talking to your prescriber first. They know your full medication list and can weigh this up for you. If you are struggling with your mood, please use [get help](/get-help/).
