Passionflower and chamomile are both mild, well-tolerated herbs for winding down at night, and neither is a clear winner for sleep. Each rests on small, encouraging trials rather than strong proof. Passionflower has a little sleep-specific research and tends to suit a busy, racing mind at bedtime, while chamomile is the classic bedtime tea with a modest record for sleep quality. For occasional restlessness either is a reasonable choice; for genuine, ongoing insomnia, neither replaces proper sleep habits or medical advice.

Because both are gentle wind-down aids rather than sedative drugs, the useful comparison is fit and routine, not one herb beating the other. The [sleep guide](/guides/sleep/) sets out where these calming herbs sit alongside habits and other supports.

## Two calming herbs with different traditions

Passionflower (*Passiflora incarnata*) has been used traditionally to ease nervous tension and support sleep, and its proposed mechanism involves supporting GABA activity, the brain's main calming signal. Chamomile (*Matricaria chamomilla*) is the daisy-family herb behind the classic bedtime tea; its calming reputation is often linked to apigenin, a compound that binds to the same broad family of calming receptors. Both act gently, which is why they show up in teas and blends rather than as strong single-dose sedatives.

The research profiles differ in emphasis. Passionflower's small trials have looked at both short-term situational anxiety and, in at least one study, sleep quality directly. Chamomile's better-known trials focused on daytime anxiety and general sleep quality in specific groups such as older adults and new mothers. In practice, passionflower is often reached for when the problem is a mind that will not switch off, and chamomile as a steady, low-effort nightly ritual.

The choice is less about potency and more about which routine you will actually keep, and whether your main issue is a busy mind or simply wanting a calming pre-sleep habit.

## What the studies actually found

A small study by [Ngan and Conduit (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1002/ptr.3400) found that a week of nightly passionflower tea produced a modest improvement in subjective sleep quality compared with placebo in healthy adults. Passionflower's calming effect also appears in anxiety research, such as a trial by [Movafegh and colleagues (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1213/ane.0b013e318172c3f9) using it before surgery. On the chamomile side, a randomised study by [Zick and colleagues (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1186/1472-6882-11-78) tested chamomile extract for chronic insomnia and found it well tolerated with only modest objective effects, while a later meta-analysis by [Hieu and colleagues (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1002/ptr.7058) reported chamomile improved sleep quality across several trials. The [NCCIH chamomile page](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/chamomile) and [Memorial Sloan Kettering's passionflower monograph](https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/passionflower) both describe the herbs as generally safe with limited but supportive evidence.

## What to look for in a passionflower supplement for sleep

Start with the label rather than the marketing. For passionflower, look for the species named as *Passiflora incarnata*, a stated amount of extract or dried herb per serving, and third-party testing for identity and purity, since botanical products vary in what they actually contain. Because the sleep research uses different preparations, from teas to tinctures, a clearly labelled and tested product matters more than a big number on the front. Choose the format you will use consistently: a tea suits a ritual, a capsule suits convenience. If you want a nightly bedtime habit with the longest tradition behind it, chamomile tea is an easy companion or alternative. The [sleep guide](/guides/sleep/) shows how to build a wind-down routine around either herb, and pairs it with the light, timing, and habit changes that do the heavier lifting for sleep. A named species with third-party testing beats a "maximum strength" claim.

## Cautions

Both herbs are generally well tolerated, but a few cautions apply. Chamomile belongs to the daisy family, so anyone allergic to ragweed, marigold, or related plants should be careful. Passionflower can add to the effect of sedatives and, like other calming herbs, should be paused before surgery. Neither is established as safe in pregnancy or breastfeeding, so avoid regular use then unless your clinician approves. If you take sedative, anti-anxiety, or sleep medication, check for additive drowsiness with your prescriber. These herbs support relaxation and a wind-down routine; they do not treat insomnia or an anxiety disorder. If sleep problems persist for weeks, or come with low mood, speak to a healthcare professional rather than relying on herbs alone.
