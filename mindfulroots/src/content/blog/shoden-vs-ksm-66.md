Shoden and KSM-66 are both branded ashwagandha extracts of the same plant, so the choice comes down to your goal rather than which label number looks bigger. KSM-66 is a root-only extract with by far the larger trial base, and it is the usual pick for daytime stress and energy. Shoden is a highly concentrated, low-dose extract with a smaller but well-designed set of studies, often used for sleep.

Because this is one herb standardised two ways, the useful comparison is about fit and evidence, not one extract beating the other. Our [ashwagandha page](/products/ashwagandha/) and the [stress guide](/guides/stress/) set out where the ingredient sits before the brand names.

## Same plant, two standardisation markers

Ashwagandha (*Withania somnifera*) is an adaptogenic herb used in Ayurvedic practice to support the body's response to stress. Its active compounds are withanolides, and a branded extract standardises them so each capsule delivers a known amount. The trap with Shoden and KSM-66 is that they standardise to different markers.

Shoden is standardised to about 35 percent withanolide glycosides, a broad family that includes sugar-bound forms, and it is dosed low, around 120 to 240 mg. KSM-66 is standardised to about 5 percent withanolides in the free, non-glycoside sense, and it is dosed high, typically 300 mg twice daily or 600 mg once. The two percentages are not measuring the same molecules, so dividing 35 by 5 tells you nothing about relative strength. A concentrated extract taken at a low dose and a lighter extract taken at a high dose can deliver comparable amounts of active compound.

## What the trials measured

KSM-66 carries the larger evidence base. A randomised trial by [Chandrasekhar and colleagues (2012)](https://doi.org/10.4103/0253-7176.106022) using a high-concentration root extract found reduced stress and lower cortisol over 60 days, and [Salve and colleagues (2019)](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.6466) reported reduced stress and morning cortisol with standardised root extract. Sleep work by [Langade and colleagues (2019)](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.5797) supports root extract for insomnia and anxiety. Shoden's headline trial, [Lopresti and colleagues (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1097/MD.0000000000017186), used a 240 mg Shoden dose standardised to withanolide glycosides and found reductions in morning cortisol and improvements in wellbeing over 60 days. An independent overview at [Examine's ashwagandha page](https://examine.com/supplements/ashwagandha/) rates the herb's stress evidence as reasonably strong overall. Both are real extracts with human data; the difference for a buyer is that KSM-66 has many more trials to match, while Shoden offers a concentrated low-dose option with promising sleep and stress results.

## What to look for in an ashwagandha extract supplement

Pick your goal first, then read the label. For daytime stress and energy with the deepest research, look for KSM-66 named specifically, a dose near 300 mg twice daily or 600 mg once, and a stated withanolide percentage of at least 5 percent. For a low-dose extract studied for sleep, look for Shoden named specifically at its studied 120–240 mg range. In both cases the label should state the brand and the standardisation marker, not just "ashwagandha extract," so you can line the product up against the trials. Prioritise third-party testing for purity, because ashwagandha root can carry contaminants when sourcing is poor. Our [ashwagandha page](/products/ashwagandha/) lays out the evidence and typical use, and the [sleep guide](/guides/sleep/) shows how it sits beside other nighttime supports. A named brand and a stated marker tell you more than a "maximum strength" claim. For a KSM-66 root extract, our product page is the place to compare specifics.

## When the premium is not worth it

If your goal is daytime stress and you find a transparent generic that states root-only sourcing, a withanolide percentage of at least 5 percent, and a dose in the studied 300–600 mg range, it can serve well at a lower price than either branded extract. The branded premium mainly buys a dose that matches named trials, which matters most when you want to reproduce a specific study. Shoden's premium is most worth it when you specifically want a low-pill-burden, sleep-leaning extract; it is less compelling for daytime energy, where KSM-66 or a clear generic fits better. Paying extra for the bigger label percentage in the belief that 35 must beat 5 is the exact assumption to avoid, because the two numbers count different molecules.

## Cautions

Ashwagandha is generally well tolerated, but it is not for everyone. It can add to the effect of sedatives and thyroid medication, and there have been rare reports of liver issues, so anyone with a thyroid or liver condition should be cautious. Avoid it in pregnancy and breastfeeding. If you take prescription medication or manage a health condition, check with your prescriber or pharmacist before starting, whichever extract you choose. Ashwagandha supports the stress response; it does not treat an anxiety or mood disorder.
