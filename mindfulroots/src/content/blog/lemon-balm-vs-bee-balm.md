Lemon balm (*Melissa officinalis*) is a mint-family herb grown for its calming, citrus-scented leaf, while bee balm (*Monarda*) is a North American flowering plant used mainly as a pollinator flower, a tea, and a seasoning. The two get compared because their common names sound alike and both smell faintly of citrus, but they are different plants with different track records. If your interest is calm, stress, or sleep, this is not really a close contest: lemon balm carries the human research, and bee balm is largely a culinary and garden plant. Naming them as if they were rival supplements would overstate what bee balm actually is.

If calm and sleep are the goal, it is worth knowing that neither herb is the most-studied option on the shelf. Our [L-theanine page](/products/l-theanine/) covers a calming ingredient with a larger evidence base, and this post explains where lemon balm and bee balm genuinely differ so you can tell which is which.

## The quick verdict

Choose **lemon balm** if you want a calming herb with actual human research behind it for mood, stress, and sleep quality. Treat **bee balm** as a pleasant culinary tea and pollinator plant, not a supplement for anxiety or sleep, because the clinical evidence for it barely exists. They are different species doing different jobs, so the honest answer to "which is better" depends entirely on what you are trying to do.

## Lemon balm: the calming herb with research

Lemon balm has been used since antiquity to lift mood and settle nerves, and modern work backs up part of that reputation. A [2004 study by Kennedy and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15272110/) found that standardised lemon balm extract improved calmness and altered stress responses to a laboratory task, in a dose-dependent way. Memorial Sloan Kettering's [About Herbs summary of lemon balm](https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/lemon-balm) notes traditional use for anxiety and sleep and points to small human studies, while flagging that the evidence base is still modest rather than definitive.

The plausible mechanism involves rosmarinic acid and effects on GABA activity, which fits the calming, non-sedating feel people describe. This places lemon balm at an emerging-to-moderate evidence tier for calm: promising small trials and a reasonable mechanism, not large confirmatory studies. It is usually taken as a standardised leaf extract, sometimes paired with other calming herbs.

## Bee balm: mostly a garden and kitchen plant

Bee balm, the *Monarda* genus, is a showy North American wildflower long grown to attract bees and hummingbirds. Indigenous peoples and later settlers brewed its leaves as a tea, sometimes called Oswego tea, and it is still used to flavour dishes and drinks. Its aromatic oils are rich in thymol, the same compound found in thyme, which has drawn some laboratory interest for antimicrobial activity.

What bee balm does not have is a body of human research for stress, anxiety, or sleep. There are no standardised extracts, no established dose, and little formal safety data beyond its long history as an occasional tea. That history is reassuring for casual culinary use, but it is not the same as evidence that it helps you sleep or feel calmer. Treating it as a calming supplement asks it to do a job it has never really been tested for.

## What to look for in a lemon balm supplement for calm and sleep

If lemon balm is the direction you want, a few criteria separate a useful product from a vague one. Favour a **standardised Melissa officinalis extract** that states a marker such as rosmarinic acid content, so the active level is defined rather than a plain "lemon balm leaf." Match the **dose to what studies used**, often 300 to 600 mg of extract, rather than guessing from a tea bag. Choose **third-party tested** products for label accuracy, and check whether the product is lemon balm alone or a blend, since blends hide how much of each herb you are getting. ## Cautions

Lemon balm is generally well tolerated; higher doses can cause mild drowsiness or stomach upset, and because it may affect thyroid signalling and sedation, anyone on thyroid or sedative medication, or who is pregnant, should check with a clinician first. Bee balm has little formal safety data as a supplement, so keep it to occasional culinary use and be cautious in pregnancy or alongside medication. Neither herb treats an anxiety or sleep disorder; they offer gentle support at most. If poor sleep or ongoing stress is affecting your life, talk to a clinician.
