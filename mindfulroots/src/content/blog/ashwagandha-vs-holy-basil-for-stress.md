For general, everyday stress, ashwagandha is the stronger first choice: it has the deeper and more consistent human evidence for lowering perceived stress and cortisol. Holy basil, or tulsi, is worth trying when stress travels with low mood or mental fatigue. Both are adaptogenic herbs, not quick fixes, and both need a few weeks to judge. Adaptogens are herbs proposed to help the body resist and recover from stress by acting on the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, the system that governs the cortisol response. Ashwagandha (*Withania somnifera*) and holy basil (*Ocimum sanctum*, tulsi) are two of the most used, but they arrive at "stress support" from different traditions and with very different depths of evidence.

## How each herb is thought to work

Ashwagandha's active withanolides are proposed to dampen an over-active HPA axis and reduce cortisol output, which lines up with the calmer, slightly sedating feel most people describe. Holy basil contains eugenol, ursolic acid, and other compounds studied for anti-stress and adaptogenic effects, and its traditional use spans stress, mood, and metabolic support. A review of tulsi by [Cohen (2014)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25624701/) describes this broad adaptogenic profile, while the [NCCIH overview of ashwagandha](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ashwagandha) sets out its use as a stress and sleep support with a caveat that longer-term safety is less certain.

## What the human evidence shows

This is where the two separate. Ashwagandha has the deeper clinical base: a randomised, placebo-controlled trial by [Chandrasekhar and colleagues (2012)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23439798/) found a high-concentration root extract significantly reduced perceived stress scores and serum cortisol over 60 days, and a later KSM-66 trial by [Salve and colleagues (2019)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32021735/) reported reduced stress and morning cortisol. Holy basil's human evidence is thinner but real: a systematic review by [Jamshidi and Cohen (2017)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28400848/) found that across the available clinical studies tulsi improved stress, anxiety-related symptoms, and metabolic markers, while noting the trials were small and varied. The honest read: ashwagandha is better proven for stress and cortisol, holy basil is promising and suits the stress-plus-low-mood picture.

## What to look for in an ashwagandha supplement for stress relief

Match the product to what was tested. For **ashwagandha**, the deepest trial record belongs to root-only standardised extracts — KSM-66 and Sensoril are the two most studied — at 300–600 mg a day, standardised to a stated withanolide percentage. Choose a single-ingredient product with third-party testing for identity and contaminants, and skip mega-dose "stress blends" that stack stimulants or sedatives you cannot dose separately. For **holy basil**, look for a standardised leaf extract stating its dose per serving rather than a raw powder of unknown strength. ## Cautions and who should be careful

Both herbs are generally well tolerated short term, with mild stomach upset the usual complaint. Ashwagandha can raise thyroid hormone levels and has rare reports of liver injury, so anyone with a thyroid or liver condition, an autoimmune disease, or a pregnancy should avoid it or seek advice first. Holy basil may lower blood sugar and has traditional cautions around fertility, so people on diabetes medication or trying to conceive should be careful. Both can add to sedatives. Because these are your-money-or-your-life decisions, talk to your prescriber before starting either herb, especially if you take regular medication.
