Saffron extract is a concentrated preparation of the Crocus sativus flower's stigmas, standardised to active compounds such as crocin and safranal, and studied mainly for mood support. So how long does it take to work? The honest answer from the trials is **six to eight weeks for a fair test, with some early signals around four weeks**. Saffron is not a same-day mood lifter. Its benefits in studies built gradually over daily dosing, which means patience and a consistent dose matter more than any single capsule.

If you are choosing a product, our [saffron extract hub](/products/saffron-extract/) covers standardisation and typical doses. This post sets out the realistic timeline, the dose behind it, and why the wait exists.

## The realistic timeline

Nearly every saffron mood trial ran for six to eight weeks and measured its main outcome at the end of that window. That is the timeframe to hold yourself to before judging it.

A [2019 systematic review and meta-analysis by Marx and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1093/nutrit/nuz023) pooled randomised trials of saffron for depression and anxiety symptoms and found a consistent benefit, with studies typically running six to eight weeks. Standardised branded extracts have shown faster early signals: a [2017 trial by Kell and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ctim.2017.08.024) using affron in adults with low mood reported improvements in mood and stress measures over four weeks at 28 mg per day. So four weeks is a reasonable checkpoint, but eight weeks is the fair verdict.

The takeaway is simple. If saffron is doing something for you, you will usually know by six to eight weeks. If nothing has shifted by then at a studied dose, it probably is not your answer.

## The dose behind the timeline

The timeline only holds at the dose the studies used. Most saffron mood trials used **30 mg per day of a standardised extract**, frequently split into two 15 mg doses. Branded standardised forms such as affron are studied at around 28 mg daily.

| Study | Design | Dose and length | Key finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| Marx 2019 | Meta-analysis | 30 mg/day, 6–8 weeks | Reduced depression and anxiety scores |
| Kell 2017 | RCT, 121 adults | 28 mg/day affron, 4 weeks | Better mood and stress measures |
| Lopresti 2018 | RCT, adolescents | 28 mg/day, 8 weeks | Youth-reported mood improvement |

Higher doses do not buy a faster result and are not risk-free. Saffron is safe in the milligram doses used in supplements, but it becomes toxic in gram amounts, so chasing a bigger dose to speed things up is both pointless and unwise. Stay near 30 mg and give it the weeks it needs. This places saffron at a moderate evidence tier for mood support: several positive trials and a supportive meta-analysis, though most studies are small and short.

## What to look for in a standardised saffron extract supplement

Because the timeline depends on getting a real, standardised dose, the label is where the decision is made. Look for an extract that states its **crocin or safranal content**, the markers of a genuine standardised preparation, rather than plain "saffron powder" with no numbers. Match the studied amount: around **28 to 30 mg per day**, so you are reproducing a trial dose rather than guessing. A **clinically studied branded form such as affron** carries the most human data and is a reasonable quality signal. Insist on **third-party testing**, because saffron is one of the most adulterated botanicals, often cut with cheaper plant material or dyes. Avoid vague "mood blends" that hide the saffron dose among other herbs, since you cannot tell whether you are taking a studied amount. ## Cautions and interactions

Saffron is well tolerated at supplement doses, but a few points deserve care. Mild side effects can include nausea, headache, or changes in appetite. Because its studied effects touch serotonin-related pathways, people taking antidepressants such as SSRIs should not add saffron without medical advice, since the combination has not been well tested for safety. Saffron may also affect blood pressure and could add to the effect of blood-thinning medication.

Pregnancy is a firm reason to avoid supplemental saffron, as higher doses have traditionally been linked to pregnancy risk. The [Memorial Sloan Kettering integrative medicine summary](https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/saffron) notes these cautions and the lack of long-term safety data. If low mood has lasted more than a couple of weeks or is affecting your daily life, saffron is not a substitute for proper care.
