If your stress is really acute demand, a deadline, an exam, or a rough night with too little sleep, L-tyrosine is the more logical pick because it feeds the brain chemicals you burn through under short-term pressure. If your stress is the ongoing, wound-up kind that wears you down over weeks, ashwagandha has the better evidence and works by calming the body's stress-hormone response. They are not interchangeable, so the useful move is to match the supplement to what your stress actually feels like.

You can read more on the [ashwagandha page](/products/ashwagandha/), and the [stress guide](/guides/stress/) sets both among other supports. One is an amino acid you take on the day; the other is a daily habit you give several weeks.

## How each one works

L-tyrosine is an amino acid the body uses to make dopamine, noradrenaline and adrenaline, the messengers that get depleted when you are under acute stress or short on sleep. Supplementing it gives the brain more raw material to keep those chemicals topped up when demand is high. A 2015 review by [Jongkees and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26424423/) concluded that tyrosine supports working memory and mental flexibility specifically when a stressor or heavy cognitive load has drained those neurotransmitters, and shows little effect in relaxed conditions. An earlier study by [Neri and colleagues (1995)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7794222/) found tyrosine reduced the drop in performance during a night of sleep deprivation.

Ashwagandha (*Withania somnifera*) works on the other end of the problem. It is an adaptogen whose withanolide compounds are associated with a lower cortisol response, the hormone that climbs under sustained stress. A randomised, placebo-controlled trial by [Chandrasekhar and colleagues (2012)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23439798/) reported lower stress scores and reduced serum cortisol over 60 days, and a later trial by [Salve and colleagues (2019)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31728244/) found similar reductions in stress and morning cortisol with a standardised root extract. So ashwagandha addresses stress by dialling down the load, not by fuelling a sprint.

## Head-to-head

| | L-tyrosine | Ashwagandha |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | Amino acid, dopamine precursor | Adaptogenic herb |
| Best for | Acute stress, sleep loss, mental load | Ongoing stress, tension, poor recovery |
| Onset | Within hours, taken on demand | Builds over 8 to 12 weeks |
| Typical dose | 500 to 2,000 mg before the task | 300 to 600 mg standardised extract daily |
| Evidence tier | Emerging, task-specific | Moderate for stress and cortisol |

The pattern is clear enough that stacking them makes sense for some people: ashwagandha as a daily base for background stress, L-tyrosine reserved for the days that ask more of you. But there is no need to force that. Pick the single one that matches your main complaint first.

## What to look for in an ashwagandha supplement for stress and focus

If ongoing stress is your issue and you want a supplement that also keeps you sharp rather than sedated, ashwagandha is the more studied choice, and a few label checks separate a useful product from filler. Look for a standardised root extract that states its withanolide percentage, so you know the active content rather than guessing from raw powder weight. Check the dose per serving lands in the 300 to 600 mg range used across the trials above, since underdosed capsules are common. Third-party testing matters for a root extract, and a single-ingredient version keeps things simple if you want to judge its effect on its own. The [ashwagandha page](/products/ashwagandha/) covers what to check, and the [stress guide](/guides/stress/) sets it against other options if you are still deciding. L-tyrosine, by contrast, is a plain amino acid where the main things to confirm are dose and purity.

## Cautions and interactions

Ashwagandha may not suit you if you have a thyroid condition, since it can nudge thyroid hormone levels, or if you are pregnant, and it should be used cautiously alongside sedatives or thyroid medication. L-tyrosine is generally well tolerated but can interact with thyroid medication and with MAOI antidepressants, and people with an overactive thyroid should be careful. Neither is a treatment for depression or an anxiety disorder; both are support for everyday stress. If you take any prescription medication or have a health condition, talk to your prescriber before starting either.
