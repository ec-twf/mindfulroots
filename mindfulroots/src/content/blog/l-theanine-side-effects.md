L-theanine is one of the best-tolerated calming supplements you can buy, and serious side effects are rare. When people do notice something it is usually mild — a slight headache, a little dizziness, or some drowsiness — and it tends to show up at higher doses rather than the 100–200 mg used in most studies. The one thing worth flagging to a doctor is a possible mild drop in blood pressure. The [L-theanine product page](/products/l-theanine/) covers how to choose one, and the [anxiety guide](/guides/anxiety/) sets it among other calming options.

## What L-theanine is, and why its side-effect profile is mild

L-theanine (γ-glutamylethylamide) is an amino acid found almost exclusively in tea leaves; it is the compound that makes green tea feel calming rather than jittery. Taken as a supplement, it promotes a state of relaxed alertness without sedation. Because it is a water-soluble amino acid that the body handles much as it handles the amino acids in food, it has a wide safety margin. A comprehensive review by [Türközü and Şanlıer (2017)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26192072/) and the reference overview on [Examine's L-theanine page](https://examine.com/supplements/l-theanine/) both describe it as well tolerated, with adverse effects that are uncommon and dose-related rather than a consistent pattern.

## The side effects that do get reported

The effects people occasionally report are minor. Headache is the one that comes up most, usually mild and short-lived. Some people notice lightheadedness or a faint drowsiness, especially at doses above the studied range or when they are already tired. Digestive upset is unusual. None of these are dangerous, and they typically ease when the dose drops. In the four-week randomised trial by [Hidese and colleagues (2019)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31623400/), 30 healthy adults took 200 mg a day and the supplement was well tolerated, with no meaningful difference in adverse events versus placebo. This matches the wider picture: L-theanine's Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) status in the United States reflects a long record of routine use without a signal of harm at normal intakes.

## The one interaction worth knowing: blood pressure

The most practical caution is not really a "side effect" but an interaction. Part of how L-theanine eases the physical side of stress is a mild dampening of the blood-pressure rise that stress produces, shown in the stress-response work summarised on [Examine's L-theanine page](https://examine.com/supplements/l-theanine/). In a healthy person this is small and harmless. The concern is additive: if you already take blood-pressure-lowering medication, the two effects could stack and leave you feeling dizzy or faint. That is a conversation to have with your doctor or pharmacist rather than a reason to avoid theanine outright. There is no evidence that L-theanine is habit-forming or that stopping it causes withdrawal.

## What to look for in a single-ingredient L-theanine supplement

If you want the cleanest safety profile, the label choices matter more than the brand. Look for a product where L-theanine is the only active ingredient, with the milligrams per capsule stated clearly so you can start at the low end of the 100–200 mg studied range. The branded Suntheanine® form is the one used in most published trials, so a label that names it is a reasonable provenance signal; third-party testing (NSF, USP, or Informed Choice) is the other. Avoid "calm", "focus" or "sleep" blends that stack several botanicals, because each addition widens the list of possible interactions and makes any reaction harder to trace back to one cause. The [L-theanine page](/products/l-theanine/) lists what to verify, and the [sleep guide](/guides/sleep/) shows where a single-ingredient option fits.

## Who should be cautious

Most healthy adults can use L-theanine without special precautions, but a few groups should check first:

- Anyone taking blood-pressure medication, because of the possible additive lowering effect.
- People who are pregnant or breastfeeding, where safety data is limited.
- Anyone taking medication for a mental-health or neurological condition — not because a specific dangerous interaction is established, but because any new supplement should be cleared with the prescriber.
- Children, unless a clinician is guiding use.

L-theanine is support for everyday stress and focus, not a treatment for anxiety or any diagnosed condition, and it is not a substitute for care. If you are unsure whether it fits with a medication you take, ask your pharmacist first.
