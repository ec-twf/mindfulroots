If you want the option with actual human evidence behind it, valerian root is the clearer pick, because it has been tested in real sleep trials even if the results are mixed. If you are drawn to catnip, it is best understood as a gentle, traditional relaxant with very little clinical research to lean on. So this is not an even contest on evidence: valerian has been studied in people, and catnip mostly has not. Both are mild, and neither is a strong sedative, which is worth knowing before you expect either to solve serious sleep trouble.

## How each one works

Valerian (*Valeriana officinalis*) is a root long used for sleep and restlessness. Its proposed mechanism centres on GABA, the main calming neurotransmitter, with compounds in the root thought to increase GABA activity or slow its breakdown. The exact active ingredient is still debated, which is part of why results vary so much between products and studies.

Catnip (*Nepeta cataria*) is a mint-family herb best known for the reaction it causes in cats, driven by a compound called nepetalactone. In people it has been used as a mild relaxing tea for generations. The trouble is that its human effect is gentle, hard to measure, and barely studied in controlled settings, so most of what is said about catnip for sleep rests on tradition and animal work rather than trials.

## What the evidence actually shows

Valerian has the more serious research record, though it is genuinely mixed. A [2006 systematic review and meta-analysis by Bent and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17145239/) found that valerian might improve subjective sleep quality, while cautioning that the trials were of uneven quality and the objective data were weak. A later [2010 meta-analysis by Fernández-San-Martín and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20347389/) reached a similar conclusion: a possible benefit for sleep quality, with methodological limits across the studies. The US [National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health summary of valerian](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/valerian) sums it up as inconsistent evidence and generally good short-term tolerability.

Catnip cannot point to anything comparable. There is no body of controlled human sleep trials for it, so any claim about catnip as a sleep aid should be read as traditional use rather than tested fact. That absence is not proof it does nothing, but it does mean valerian wins the evidence comparison by default.

## Cautions for both herbs

Neither valerian nor catnip is a strong sedative, but both can add to drowsiness, so avoid pairing them with alcohol, sleeping pills, or other sedating medication without advice. Valerian is not recommended in pregnancy or breastfeeding due to limited safety data, and some people notice grogginess or vivid dreams. Catnip is generally treated as low-risk in small amounts but is likewise best avoided in pregnancy. If sleep problems are persistent, severe, or paired with low mood, that is a reason to speak with a clinician rather than to keep trialling herbs.
