Ashwagandha (*Withania somnifera*) is an adaptogenic herb taken to support the body's response to stress, while sertraline is a prescription SSRI antidepressant. People ask whether the two can share a shelf because both touch mood and stress, and the herb is sold freely while the drug is not. The short answer is that the combination is usually considered low-risk, but "usually low-risk" is not the same as "no need to check." Ashwagandha can add to sertraline's sedation, shift thyroid hormones, and, rarely, affect the liver, so this is a plan to run past the clinician who prescribed your medication.

If you are weighing ashwagandha for stress support, our [ashwagandha hub](/products/ashwagandha/) covers what the standardised extracts are and how they are dosed. This post is about the interaction question specifically, so you know what the real concerns are before you raise it with your prescriber.

## The honest risk picture

The worry people bring to this question is serotonin syndrome, the dangerous build-up of serotonin activity that can happen when serotonergic drugs are combined. That risk is real for things like 5-HTP, St John's wort, and tramadol added to an SSRI. Ashwagandha is different. It is not a serotonin precursor and does not block serotonin reuptake, so there is no strong mechanism for it to push serotonin the way those agents do. The US [National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ashwagandha) describes ashwagandha as generally well tolerated in short-term studies and does not flag a serotonin interaction, and there is no pattern of case reports pairing it with SSRIs for that reaction.

That does not make the combination entirely free of edges. The practical concerns are additive sedation, thyroid effects, and occasional liver reactions, covered below.

## Sedation adds up

Ashwagandha has a calming, mildly sedating character, which is much of why people take it in the evening. Sertraline can also cause drowsiness or fatigue, particularly in the first weeks. Stacked together, they may leave you more tired or slowed than either does alone. This is rarely dangerous, but it matters if you drive, operate machinery, or already feel flattened by your medication. Starting ashwagandha at a low dose, and not in the morning before a demanding day, makes any added drowsiness easier to notice and manage.

## Thyroid and liver: the effects worth watching

Ashwagandha can raise thyroid hormone levels. A [2018 randomized trial by Sharma and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28829155/) found that root extract increased T3 and T4 and lowered TSH in people with subclinical hypothyroidism. That can be helpful or unhelpful depending on your thyroid status, and it is a reason for anyone on thyroid medication, or being monitored for mood-related thyroid changes, to loop in their clinician.

Liver reactions are rare but documented. The NIH [LiverTox database](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK548536/) records a small number of cases of liver injury linked to ashwagandha-containing products, usually reversible after stopping. Sertraline is also processed by the liver, so if you notice unusual fatigue, dark urine, or yellowing of the skin or eyes, stop and seek care. Ashwagandha can additionally stimulate immune activity, which is a separate reason for people with autoimmune conditions to be cautious.

## What the evidence base actually is

Ashwagandha's stress evidence is moderate and growing. A frequently cited [2012 randomized, placebo-controlled trial by Chandrasekhar and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23439798/) reported lower perceived-stress scores and morning cortisol with a standardised root extract over 60 days. Memorial Sloan Kettering's [About Herbs summary of ashwagandha](https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/ashwagandha) reaches a similar read: reasonable short-term stress data, with drug-interaction information still limited. What is missing is direct research on ashwagandha taken alongside sertraline, which is exactly why prescriber judgment fills the gap rather than a published combination study.

## What to look for in an ashwagandha supplement if you take sertraline

If your prescriber is comfortable with a trial, a few criteria make the choice safer and easier to judge. Favour a **standardised root extract** with a named form such as **KSM-66 or Sensoril** and a stated withanolide percentage, so you know what you are actually taking rather than a vague "ashwagandha powder." Match the **dose to what trials used** (commonly 300 mg twice daily for KSM-66-style extracts) rather than the highest number on the shelf, and **start at the low end** so any extra sedation is easy to spot against your sertraline. Choose a **third-party tested** product for label accuracy, since herbal supplements vary. Avoid combination "stress blends" that hide the ashwagandha dose among other actives, which makes side effects hard to trace. ## The bottom line: ask your prescriber

Ashwagandha and sertraline are not a known dangerous pair, and many people combine a stress adaptogen with an SSRI without trouble. But sertraline is a prescription medicine treating something that matters, ashwagandha genuinely affects thyroid hormones and occasionally the liver, and no study has tested the exact combination. That is enough to make this a decision you share with the clinician who knows your history, not one you make alone. Keep taking your sertraline exactly as prescribed, bring the idea of adding ashwagandha to your prescriber or pharmacist, and let them give it the yes or no.
