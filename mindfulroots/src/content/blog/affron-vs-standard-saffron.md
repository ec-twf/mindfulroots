affron and standard saffron extract come from the same flower, so the branded version is not a stronger or different herb. What the premium actually buys is a defined, tested standardisation and a set of clinical trials run on that exact extract at 28 mg a day. If you want the product that matches the mood research, affron is the safer bet; if budget leads, a reputable generic saffron extract standardised for its active compounds is a fair alternative. ## The honest starting point: same plant

Saffron is the dried stigma of *Crocus sativus*, and its mood-relevant compounds are crocins and safranal. affron is not a competing botanical or a novel molecule; it is a saffron extract, standardised and trademarked by a single manufacturer. So the question is not "which herb is better" but "what does the trademark add to the same herb". The answer is two things: a fixed standardisation, and a body of trials run on that specific extract. Everything below is about whether those two things are worth paying for.

## What the evidence actually shows

Standardised saffron has moderate evidence for supporting low mood. For affron specifically, a trial by [Kell and colleagues (2017)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28511781/) reported improvements in mood measures in healthy adults with low mood at 28 mg per day, and a study in adolescents by [Lopresti and colleagues (2018)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29397794/) found improvements in some anxiety and depression scores at the same dose. For saffron in general, a meta-analysis by [Toth and colleagues (2019)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30036891/) concluded that saffron supplementation was associated with reduced depressive symptoms across trials, and Memorial Sloan Kettering's [saffron monograph](https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/saffron) summarises the human data and safety. The practical read: the plant has a real evidence base, and affron's contribution is that some of that base was generated on the exact extract you can buy.

## What to look for: affron or generic saffron extract, which to buy

The decision comes down to how much you value matching the studied product versus saving money:

- **Standardisation stated on the label.** affron gives 3.5% Lepticrosalides; a good generic will state a safranal or crocin percentage. No standardisation stated is a reason to walk away, because saffron is heavily adulterated.
- **A 28 to 30 mg daily dose**, ideally split, to match the trials.
- **Third-party testing for identity and adulterants**, which matters more for saffron than almost any other botanical.
- **Single-ingredient or clean formulation**, so you can attribute effects and control the dose.

## When the premium is not worth it

This is the part every page selling saffron leaves out. Because affron and generic saffron are the same molecule, the premium is not worth it when you can find a generic extract that states a real standardisation, gives the 28 to 30 mg clinical dose, and carries third-party testing for adulteration. In that case you are buying the same active compounds at the same effective dose. The premium *is* worth it when the generic option is vague: no standardisation, no testing, and a suspiciously low price, which for a botanical this commonly faked means you may not be getting much saffron at all. You are paying affron for certainty and traceability, not for a different or stronger herb, and if a generic can offer comparable certainty, the honest answer is that it will do.

## Cautions and interactions

Saffron is generally well tolerated at these doses, but it modulates serotonin, so combining it with an SSRI, SNRI, or other serotonergic medication should be cleared with your prescriber rather than done on your own. High doses far above the supplemental range can be toxic. Saffron is best avoided in pregnancy at supplemental doses. It supports mood; it does not treat clinical depression, and it should not replace prescribed care.
