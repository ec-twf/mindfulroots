Timing advice for supplements reads as contradictory because the question is usually asked about one ingredient at a time. Across a dozen of them a simpler structure appears. Three things decide when to take something, and only the third is about the clock.

## The three questions

**Does it act in an hour, or over weeks?** This decides whether the hour matters at all. L-theanine and 5-HTP act within about an hour and clear within a few, so you time them to the moment you want to feel something. Ashwagandha and a B-complex work cumulatively, so the hour is close to irrelevant and the only variable that matters is whether you take it at all. A dose taken daily with breakfast beats a perfectly timed dose you skip half the week.

**Is it fat-soluble?** This decides the food question, and only two of these supplements genuinely care. Vitamin D3 and fish oil need a meal containing fat to absorb properly. L-theanine and the B vitamins are water-soluble and absorb fine without one.

**Does it push you up or down?** This is the only part that is really about morning versus night. 5-HTP and ashwagandha lean sedating, so evening suits them. B vitamins and rhodiola lean activating, so morning does.

## L-theanine: time it to the moment

L-theanine is the clearest case where the hour is the whole decision, because the effect arrives within about an hour and fades over a few rather than accumulating day to day. That makes it the one supplement here you can genuinely use on demand, 30 to 60 minutes before something stressful, as well as at a fixed daily time for background calm.

Morning suits calm, steady focus or smoothing out coffee jitters. Evening suits winding down. People who take it twice often do both, and that is not a contradiction, because each dose is doing a separate job. The trial work spans both patterns: [Hidese and colleagues (2019)](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu11102362) dosed 200 mg daily for four weeks, while [Kimura and colleagues (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsycho.2006.06.006) and [Nobre and colleagues (2008)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18296328/) measured effects inside a single session. Pharmacokinetic work by [Scheid and colleagues (2012)](https://doi.org/10.3945/jn.112.166371) and [Lu and colleagues (2004)](https://doi.org/10.1002/hup.611) is what makes the short window reliable enough to plan around.

Food is optional. L-theanine is a water-soluble amino acid, so unlike a fat-soluble vitamin it does not need dietary fat to be absorbed ([Williams et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11130-019-00771-5)). A light snack only matters if a dose leaves your stomach feeling off. See the [L-theanine page](/products/l-theanine/).

## 5-HTP: evening, with a snack, and one hard rule first

Before any timing question: if you take an SSRI, SNRI, MAOI, tricyclic, triptan, tramadol, dextromethorphan or St John's wort, 5-HTP needs a prescriber's sign-off rather than a schedule, because the serotonin syndrome risk outranks everything on this page. See [5-HTP and serotonin safety](/blog/5-htp-serotonin-safety/).

Past that, evening with a small amount of food is the practical default. Serotonin's role in sleep makes evening a reasonable fit for wind-down, and the short half-life described by [Birdsall (1998)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9727088/) and the review by [Turner and colleagues (2006)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pharmthera.2005.06.004) means it does not carry into the next day. A morning or split dose can work if you are after daytime mood rather than sleep.

The food part is not about absorption. Nausea is 5-HTP's most common side effect, worst in the first few weeks and at higher doses, and a light snack usually settles it. Some people prefer dosing between meals so it competes less with dietary protein, but for comfort, food wins. A low starting dose around 50 mg does more for tolerability than any timing choice ([MSKCC monograph](https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/5-htp); [Maffei, 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms22010181)). See the [5-HTP page](/products/5-htp/).

## Ashwagandha: any hour, every day, with a meal

Ashwagandha is the clearest case of the opposite kind, where the hour is a preference and the daily habit is the whole thing. It builds gradually over weeks rather than acting like a fast sedative, so a single well-timed dose achieves nothing a badly timed one would not.

Most of the stress trials dosed it twice a day with meals, which makes splitting it morning and evening a defensible default: [Chandrasekhar and colleagues (2012)](https://doi.org/10.4103/0253-7176.106022), [Lopresti and colleagues (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1097/MD.0000000000017186), [Salve and colleagues (2019)](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.6466) and the sleep-focused trial by [Langade and colleagues (2019)](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.5797). Choose evening if sleep is the goal, morning if daytime stress and focus are. The [NCCIH overview](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ashwagandha) and the [MSKCC entry](https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/ashwagandha) both treat the schedule as secondary.

Take it with a meal regardless, since stomach upset is the usual complaint. See the [ashwagandha page](/products/ashwagandha/) and [how long ashwagandha takes to work](/blog/how-long-supplements-take-to-work/), which is the question that actually determines whether you stick with it.

## B-complex: morning, with breakfast

Morning with breakfast is the better default, for two reasons that have nothing to do with absorption. B vitamins are water-soluble and absorb fine on an empty stomach; the meal is there because a dose without food makes some people queasy. And a small number of people feel slightly wired from B vitamins, which is a second argument against late-evening doses.

The daytime slot also matches when the body draws on these pathways, since B vitamins work inside energy metabolism and neurotransmitter synthesis rather than producing a felt effect you are scheduling around ([Kennedy, 2016](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu8020068)). Several of them, B12 and folate in particular, operate over long periods rather than in a quick hit, so consistency outweighs the hour by a wide margin. See the NIH ODS fact sheets for [B12](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB12-HealthProfessional/), [folate](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Folate-HealthProfessional/) and [vitamin B6](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB6-HealthProfessional/), and the [B-complex page](/products/b-complex/) for which forms to look for on a label, which matters more than the schedule.

## Fish oil: the meal, not the clock

Neither morning nor night changes fish oil absorption. What changes it is a meal containing fat, and the effect is large enough to be the only timing decision worth making. [Lawson and Hughes (1988)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3196303/) showed absorption rising substantially with a fat-containing meal, and the review by [Schuchardt and Hahn (2013)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23473494/) explains why: these are triglycerides, and they follow the same digestion route as dietary fat. So pick your largest, fattiest meal and stay with it.

The one genuinely clock-related reason people shift fish oil to the evening is fishy burps, and sleeping through the repeat is a reasonable workaround rather than an absorption strategy. See the [NIH ODS omega-3 fact sheet](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-HealthProfessional/) and the [omega-3 page](/products/omega-3-fish-oil/).

## The two with their own pages

Vitamin D3 and magnesium glycinate are the two timing questions people actually search in volume, so each keeps a dedicated page.

For vitamin D3 the answer is the same shape as fish oil, since both are fat-dependent: the meal beats the clock, and an empty-stomach dose wastes part of it. [Best time to take vitamin D3](/blog/vitamin-d3-morning-or-night/) covers the absorption evidence, whether bedtime dosing affects sleep, and the empty-stomach question.

For magnesium glycinate, evening is the usual default because it is gentle and mildly calming, which suits wind-down and the sleep support most people want it for, though a morning portion is reasonable for daytime tension. [Magnesium glycinate morning or night](/blog/magnesium-glycinate-morning-or-night/) covers the dose split and the antibiotic spacing rule.

## What actually goes wrong with timing

Two mistakes account for most of it, and neither is picking the wrong hour.

The first is taking a fat-dependent supplement on an empty stomach. Vitamin D3 and fish oil are the two affected, and an early-morning dose with water is the common way it happens. This is the one timing error with a measurable cost.

The second is building a schedule too complicated to keep. Four separate dosing times is worse than one, because the cumulative supplements on this page only work if taken daily, and a split schedule is the one most likely to be abandoned. Batching everything onto your largest fat-containing meal gets the absorption question right and the adherence question right at the same time.

The one real spacing rule: keep magnesium a couple of hours away from tetracycline or quinolone antibiotics and bisphosphonates, because it reduces absorption of those drugs ([NIH ODS magnesium fact sheet](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Magnesium-HealthProfessional/)).
