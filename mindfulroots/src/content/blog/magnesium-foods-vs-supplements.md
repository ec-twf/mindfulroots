For most people the real answer is both, and in that order: food first, a supplement to fill what the plate misses. Whole foods bring magnesium wrapped in fibre, potassium and plant compounds that a capsule leaves out, so they are the better foundation. The catch is that modern diets often fall short of the target anyway, which is where a modest magnesium supplement earns its place rather than replacing good eating.

If you already suspect your intake is low and want to close the gap, our [magnesium glycinate guide](/products/magnesium-glycinate/) covers forms and dosing. This post is about the prior question: when food is enough, and when it is not.

## The verdict, before the detail

Food comes first because it is more than magnesium. A handful of pumpkin seeds or a bowl of black beans delivers the mineral alongside fibre and other nutrients, and the body absorbs and regulates food magnesium well. A supplement is the right call when your diet keeps running below the mark, when the goal is stress or sleep support and you want a known dose, or when something is blocking absorption. Neither one cancels the other out.

## How much magnesium you actually need

Adult requirements sit around 310–420 mg per day depending on age and sex, as laid out in the [NIH Office of Dietary Supplements magnesium fact sheet](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Magnesium-HealthProfessional/). That is the whole-diet target, met from food plus any supplement combined. The number matters because it sets the bar both routes are trying to clear, and it explains why a food-first plan can still leave a gap worth filling.

The gap is not hypothetical. A review of national intake data found that a substantial proportion of adults consume less magnesium than recommended, a pattern the authors link partly to refined and processed foods, which lose most of their magnesium during milling ([Rosanoff et al., *Nutrition Reviews*, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1753-4887.2011.00465.x)).

## What the evidence says about supplementing

Where intake is low, topping up has measurable effects. A systematic review of magnesium supplementation reported reductions in subjective anxiety in groups prone to it, while noting the evidence base needs stronger trials ([Boyle et al., *Nutrients*, 2017](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu9050429)). Separately, an analysis of dietary data found lower magnesium intake associated with higher odds of depression in adults, consistent with the idea that the mineral matters for mood regulation ([Tarleton & Littenberg, *J Am Board Fam Med*, 2015](https://doi.org/10.3122/jabfm.2015.02.140176)). Neither finding means a supplement treats these conditions; they point to magnesium as support that is more likely to help when a shortfall exists in the first place.

## What to look for in a magnesium glycinate supplement when diet falls short

If food is not closing the gap, the point of a supplement is to add a known, gentle dose rather than to overhaul your plate. A few criteria do most of the work:

- **Elemental magnesium per serving**, stated plainly. The weight of the whole compound is larger than the magnesium it carries, so a "500 mg" capsule may provide far less elemental magnesium. For gap-filling, 100–200 mg elemental is a common starting range.
- **Form.** Magnesium glycinate (bisglycinate) is chosen for being well tolerated and easy on the stomach, which matters if oxide or citrate has upset you before.
- **Third-party testing** for purity and dose accuracy.
- **No unnecessary fillers** or megadoses you did not intend.

Keep food doing the heavy lifting and let the capsule cover the shortfall. When you compare products, read the label for a gentle glycinate dose that states its elemental magnesium, not just the headline milligrams.

## Cautions

Supplemental magnesium has an upper limit of 350 mg per day for adults set specifically for the amount added on top of food; going above it raises the risk of loose stools and cramping, per the [NIH ODS fact sheet](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Magnesium-HealthProfessional/). Magnesium can also interact with certain antibiotics, bisphosphonates and diuretics, and people with reduced kidney function should not supplement without medical oversight, since they clear magnesium less efficiently. Food magnesium does not carry the same upper-limit concern because the body regulates it.
