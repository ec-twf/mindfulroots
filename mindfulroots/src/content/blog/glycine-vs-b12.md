Glycine and vitamin B12 get compared because both are sold for "feeling better," but they are not really competing for the same job. Glycine is an amino acid, and its evidence points to sleep and calm. B12 is an essential vitamin, and its value is in energy and mood when you are running low. If your problem is falling asleep or winding down, glycine is the relevant one. If it is fatigue, especially as a vegan, an older adult, or anyone with absorption trouble, B12 is what to check first. For most people this is an "and," not an "or."

If B12 is the side you care about, our [B-complex hub](/products/b-complex/) covers forms and dosing. This post lays out what each one actually does so you can tell which question you are trying to answer.

## The quick verdict

Choose **glycine** if you want a bedtime supplement for sleep quality and evening calm. Choose **B12** if you are tired and might be deficient, or if your diet is low in animal foods. They are different categories of nutrient with different mechanisms, so the honest answer to "which is better" is that it depends entirely on the symptom, and taking both is perfectly reasonable.

|---|---|---|
| What it is | Amino acid | Essential vitamin |

## Glycine: the sleep and calm side

Glycine is one of the smallest amino acids and doubles as an inhibitory neurotransmitter, which is the root of its calming reputation. The most-cited human work comes from a group of small Japanese trials. Taking about 3 grams before bed improved subjective sleep quality and reduced daytime tiredness in people with poor sleep, as reported by [Bannai and colleagues (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1479-8425.2011.00518.x).

The mechanism has some support beyond the subjective reports. A [2015 study by Kawai and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1038/npp.2014.326) found that glycine's sleep-promoting effect worked through NMDA receptors in the brain's master clock and involved a small drop in core body temperature, the same dip that naturally precedes sleep. This puts glycine at an emerging evidence tier for sleep: promising small trials with a plausible mechanism, not large confirmatory studies.

## B12: the energy and deficiency side

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is essential and cannot be made by the body, so it has to come from food or supplements. It is needed to make red blood cells, to keep the nervous system's myelin healthy, and for the methylation reactions that recycle homocysteine. A shortfall causes fatigue, brain fog, tingling, and, over time, low mood.

The mood link runs through methylation. A [2005 review by Coppen and Bolander-Gouaille](https://doi.org/10.1177/0269881105048899) laid out the evidence that low B12 and folate raise homocysteine and are associated with depression and weaker antidepressant response. The catch is that supplementing B12 helps mood mainly when you were actually low; it is not a mood booster for the already-replete. The US [NIH Office of Dietary Supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB12-HealthProfessional/) notes that vegans, older adults, and people with absorption problems are the groups most likely to be deficient. That is where B12's energy and mood payoff is real; elsewhere it is modest.

## What to look for when buying B12

Since B12 is the buy-intent side here, a few criteria matter. First, decide whether you need it at all: a blood test showing low B12 or a diet with little animal food is the signal, not general tiredness. If you do, look for a **methylcobalamin or cyanocobalamin** form; both raise levels, and cyanocobalamin is the well-studied, inexpensive default. A **B-complex** is often the better buy than isolated B12, because the B vitamins work as a group and deficiency rarely comes alone. Check the **dose against your need** rather than chasing the biggest number, since B12 is water-soluble and excess is simply excreted. Favour **third-party tested** products for label accuracy. Buy on form, real need, and testing, not on megadose marketing.

## Cautions

Glycine is generally well tolerated; higher doses can cause mild stomach upset, and its interaction data is limited, so anyone on clozapine or other CNS medication should ask a clinician first. B12 is considered safe even at high oral doses because the body excretes the excess, but persistent fatigue should never be self-treated with B12 alone. It can mask the blood signs of folate deficiency, and ongoing symptoms deserve a proper work-up. If either symptom, poor sleep or unexplained exhaustion, is affecting your life, talk to a clinician.
