KSM-66 and Sensoril are both branded ashwagandha extracts of the same plant, so the choice comes down to your goal rather than which is stronger. KSM-66 is a root-only extract with the larger trial base, and it is the usual pick for daytime stress and energy. Sensoril uses root and leaf, is standardised to a higher withanolide percentage, and leans more calming, which makes it the more common choice for evening use and sleep.

Because this is one herb standardised two ways, the useful comparison is about fit and evidence, not one extract beating the other. Our [ashwagandha page](/products/ashwagandha/) and the [stress guide](/guides/stress/) set out where the ingredient sits before brand names.

## Same plant, two extraction methods

Ashwagandha (*Withania somnifera*) is an adaptogenic herb used in Ayurvedic practice to support the body's response to stress. Its active compounds are withanolides, and a branded extract standardises them so each capsule delivers a known amount. The two big names standardise very differently.

KSM-66 is made from the root only and standardised to a lower withanolide percentage, then dosed higher by weight, typically around 300 mg twice daily or a single 600 mg dose. Sensoril uses both root and leaf, which yields a higher withanolide percentage, so it is used at a much smaller milligram dose. The leaf material and higher standardisation are why Sensoril often feels more sedating, while KSM-66 is more often taken in the day. Neither approach is "purer"; they are tuned for different ends of the same herb's range.

## What the trials measured

KSM-66 carries the larger evidence base. A randomised trial by [Chandrasekhar and colleagues (2012)](https://doi.org/10.4103/0253-7176.106022) using a high-concentration root extract found reduced stress and lower cortisol over 60 days, and later studies by [Salve and colleagues (2019)](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.6466) and [Lopresti and colleagues (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1097/MD.0000000000017186) reported stress and sleep benefits with standardised root extract. Sensoril's headline evidence includes an earlier stress trial (Auddy and colleagues, 2008) using its root-and-leaf extract, with the calming, sleep-leaning profile that shapes how it is dosed. Both are real extracts with human data; the difference for a buyer is that KSM-66 has more trials to match and Sensoril has the more sedating character. Independent summaries such as [Examine's ashwagandha page](https://examine.com/supplements/ashwagandha/) rate the herb's stress evidence as reasonably strong overall.

## What to look for in an ashwagandha extract supplement

Pick your goal first, then read the label. For daytime stress and energy with the deepest research, look for KSM-66 named specifically, a dose near 300 mg twice daily or 600 mg once, and a stated withanolide percentage of at least 5 percent. For evening calm and sleep, look for Sensoril named specifically at its lower studied dose. In both cases the label should give the plant part and the standardisation percentage, not just "ashwagandha extract," so you can line the product up against the trials. Prioritise third-party testing for purity, because ashwagandha root can carry contaminants when sourcing is poor. Our [ashwagandha page](/products/ashwagandha/) lays out the evidence and typical use, and the [stress guide](/guides/stress/) shows how it sits beside other stress supports. A named brand, a stated percentage and a studied dose tell you more than a "maximum strength" claim.

## When the premium is not worth it

If you find a generic ashwagandha that clearly states root-only sourcing, a withanolide percentage of at least 5 percent, and a dose in the studied range, it can serve a daytime stress goal well at a lower price than KSM-66. The branded premium mainly buys a dose that matches named trials, which matters most when you want to reproduce a study. Sensoril's premium is worth it when you specifically want its calming, sleep-leaning profile; it is less worth it for daytime energy, where KSM-66 or a transparent generic fits better. Paying extra for a higher withanolide percentage in the belief that it must be stronger for every goal is the assumption to avoid.

## Cautions

Ashwagandha is generally well tolerated, but it is not for everyone. It can add to the effect of sedatives and thyroid medication, and there have been rare reports of liver issues, so anyone with a thyroid or liver condition should be cautious. Avoid it in pregnancy and breastfeeding. If you take prescription medication or manage a health condition, check with your prescriber or pharmacist before starting, whichever extract you choose. Ashwagandha supports the stress response; it does not treat an anxiety or mood disorder.
