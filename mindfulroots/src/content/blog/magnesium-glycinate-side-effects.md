Most people tolerate magnesium glycinate well, and when side effects do happen they are usually mild and tied to the dose: loose stools or a bit of stomach upset, easing off when you take less. The form matters here. Glycinate is one of the gentler ways to take magnesium, so it causes fewer digestive complaints than the cheaper oxide or citrate versions. The side effect that actually deserves your attention is not digestive at all. If you have any kidney problem, magnesium can build up in the body, and that is the reason to talk to a clinician before you start. You can read what to look for on our [magnesium glycinate product page](/products/magnesium-glycinate/).

## Why the glycinate form is gentler

Magnesium glycinate, sometimes labelled magnesium bisglycinate, is elemental magnesium bound to two molecules of the amino acid glycine. That chelated structure changes how the mineral moves through the gut. Poorly absorbed forms such as magnesium oxide leave a lot of unabsorbed magnesium sitting in the bowel, which pulls in water and loosens stools; that is also why magnesium oxide and citrate are sold as laxatives. Because glycinate is better absorbed, less of it reaches the colon unabsorbed, so it is less likely to send you running. A 2017 comparison of magnesium salts found chelated and organic forms are more bioavailable than oxide ([Blancquaert et al., *Nutrients*, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28846654/)). The glycine that comes along for the ride is itself a well-tolerated amino acid the body uses routinely.

## The side effects people actually report

The common complaints are digestive and dose-dependent. Loose stools or mild diarrhoea top the list, followed by nausea or cramping if a large dose is taken on an empty stomach. The [NIH Office of Dietary Supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Magnesium-HealthProfessional/) notes that diarrhoea and gut upset are the most frequent effects of supplemental magnesium, and that they cluster at higher intakes. Most people who run into this are taking more than they need, taking it all at once, or using a poorly absorbed form. Splitting the dose, taking it with food, or simply lowering it usually resolves the problem within a day or two.

Some people take magnesium glycinate in the evening for its calming reputation. The evidence for magnesium and sleep is modest rather than strong, and a 2021 systematic review concluded the existing trials were small and low in quality, so treat sleep benefits as a possible bonus rather than a reason to push the dose up ([Mah & Pitre, *BMC Complementary Medicine and Therapies*, 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33865376/)). More is not better here.

## The dose ceiling that matters

There is a defined limit. The tolerable upper intake level for magnesium from supplements is **350 mg of elemental magnesium per day** for adults, and this is separate from the magnesium you get from food, which the body regulates safely through the kidneys. The 350 mg figure is set specifically to avoid the diarrhoea and gut effects above, not because food magnesium is dangerous. The word *elemental* is the one to watch on a label: a "500 mg" capsule often delivers far less elemental magnesium than that, so read the supplement facts panel rather than the front of the bottle. A systematic review of magnesium for anxiety and stress found the doses used in trials sat comfortably in this range and were generally well tolerated ([Boyle et al., *Nutrients*, 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28445426/)).

## When magnesium glycinate is genuinely risky

For most healthy adults the risks are minor. Two situations change that. The first is **kidney disease or reduced kidney function**: healthy kidneys clear surplus magnesium efficiently, but impaired ones cannot, so magnesium can accumulate and, in severe cases, cause dangerously low blood pressure, muscle weakness, confusion and heart rhythm disturbances. Anyone with chronic kidney disease should not take supplemental magnesium without a doctor's oversight. The second is **medication interactions**. Magnesium can reduce the absorption of certain oral antibiotics (tetracyclines and quinolones) and bisphosphonates if taken at the same time, and some diuretics and proton-pump inhibitors alter magnesium levels in the body. The NIH ODS fact sheet linked above lists these interactions in detail. Separating doses by a few hours handles most of them, but a pharmacist should confirm.

## What to look for in a gentle magnesium glycinate supplement

If a sensitive stomach is your concern, the label details matter more than the brand name. Look for a **fully chelated glycinate or bisglycinate**, not oxide, since that is the whole reason this form is easier on digestion. Check that **elemental magnesium is stated per serving**, so you can keep total supplemental intake at or under 350 mg and dose accurately. A **named chelate source** (such as an Albion-branded chelate) signals a manufacturer that controls how the mineral is bound. Finally, favour products with **third-party testing** and minimal fillers. Start at the lower end of the elemental dose, take it with food, and build up only if you tolerate it.
