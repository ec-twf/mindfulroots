Silexan is a standardised lavender essential oil, made to a fixed composition and packaged in oral capsules for anxiety support, which means the honest first thing to say about "Silexan vs lavender" is that they are not two rival herbs. Silexan *is* lavender — specifically, the one form of lavender oil that has been through randomised anxiety trials. The real comparison is between a defined, studied preparation and the wider, looser world of lavender teas, generic softgels, and aromatherapy oils, where dose and quality vary from bottle to bottle.

## The verdict, before the detail

Treat this less as a contest and more as a labelling question. **Silexan** is the version to choose if you specifically want the effect the trials describe, because the research was done on this exact oil at a defined dose. **Generic lavender** — tea, aromatherapy, an unstandardised capsule — may still be pleasant and mildly calming, but you cannot assume it matches the trial results, because it was not what the studies used.

The strongest evidence comes from work like [Kasper and colleagues (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1097/YIC.0b013e32833b3242), who found the standardised oil reduced symptoms in subsyndromal anxiety, and a later comparison by [Kasper and colleagues (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1461145714000017) that tested it against placebo and low-dose paroxetine in generalised anxiety disorder. A pooled analysis by [Möller and colleagues (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00406-017-0852-4) reached a broadly positive conclusion across trials. The consistent caveat is that much of this research was industry-funded, so the effect is best read as moderate and promising rather than definitive.

## Why the standardisation matters

Lavender oil is a mix of dozens of compounds, and the two that Silexan is standardised around — linalool and linalyl acetate — are held to a set ratio in every batch. A generic lavender softgel might contain a different oil, a different concentration, or a different plant chemotype entirely. That is the whole point of a standardised extract: it makes one bottle behave like the next, and it lets a trial result mean something for the product you actually buy. Without that standard, "lavender" is a category, not a dose.

The 80 mg once-daily capsule is the dose used across most of the Silexan studies, and the effect tends to build over a few weeks rather than working on the first dose. Lavender aromatherapy is a separate story with its own, gentler and less consistent evidence; the [NCCIH lavender overview](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/lavender) and the [Memorial Sloan Kettering lavender monograph](https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/integrative-medicine/herbs/lavender) are balanced places to read the safety and evidence detail.

## What to look for in an oral anxiety supplement

If your aim is an oral capsule with solid anxiety data and you cannot get Silexan, ashwagandha is the alternative with the deepest trial record, and the selection criteria are worth knowing. Look for a standardised root extract — KSM-66 and Sensoril are the two forms most studies used — at a daily dose in the 300 to 600 mg range, with the withanolide percentage stated on the label and third-party testing for identity and contaminants. ## Cautions and interactions

Silexan was well tolerated in trials, with mild lavender-scented burping the most common complaint. Two safety points matter more. First, it has not been well studied in pregnancy or in combination with sedative medication, so caution is warranted there. Second, an oral lavender product must be a standardised, pharmaceutical-grade capsule — swallowing raw, undiluted essential oil from an aromatherapy bottle is genuinely dangerous, as neat essential oils are toxic if ingested. This is support for mild anxiety symptoms, not a treatment for a diagnosed anxiety disorder.

If you take any prescription medication, particularly sedatives or other calming drugs, talk to your doctor or pharmacist before starting an oral lavender oil.
