For mood support, vitamin D3 is the better default. It raises and maintains blood vitamin D levels more reliably than D2, and it is the form nearly every study on vitamin D and mood has actually used. D2 still corrects a deficiency and suits people who need a strictly plant-based source, but for most people D3 is the more dependable pick. Both are "vitamin D"; the difference is how well each one gets your level up and keeps it there.

## D3 and D2 are two forms of the same vitamin

Vitamin D is a fat-soluble vitamin your body can make in skin exposed to sunlight, which is why levels fall in winter and in people who cover up or live at higher latitudes. Supplements come in two forms. D3, or cholecalciferol, is the form the body makes and is usually sourced from lanolin or fish; a lichen-derived vegan D3 also exists. D2, or ergocalciferol, is made from fungi or yeast exposed to UV light. Both are converted in the liver to 25-hydroxyvitamin D, or 25(OH)D, the marker a blood test measures, and both can raise a low level. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements [vitamin D fact sheet](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminD-HealthProfessional/) treats them as the two supplemental forms while noting they are not equally potent.

## The evidence: D3 raises levels more effectively

Where D3 and D2 differ is efficiency. A meta-analysis by [Tripkovic and colleagues (2012)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22552031/) pooled head-to-head trials and found D3 clearly more effective than D2 at raising serum 25(OH)D, an effect that grew when the vitamin was given as a single large dose. A later trial by [Logan and colleagues (2013)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23168298/) reported the same pattern over 25 weeks of daily use: D3 produced and sustained a higher blood level than an equal dose of D2. The likely reason is that D3 binds the vitamin D-binding protein more tightly and is cleared more slowly, so it lingers.

Whether raising the level lifts mood is a separate, more cautious question. A systematic review by [Anglin and colleagues (2013)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23377209/) found low vitamin D associated with a higher chance of depression, but association is not proof, and supplementation trials are mixed, with benefit concentrated in people who started out deficient. The reasonable read is to keep your level healthy rather than to expect a supplement to treat low mood.

### D3 vs D2 at a glance

| Feature | Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) | Vitamin D2 (ergocalciferol) |

## What to look for in a vitamin D3 supplement for mental health

Judge these products on specifics, not mood claims. Choose D3 (cholecalciferol) unless you specifically need a non-animal source, in which case look for lichen-derived vegan D3 rather than settling for D2. Match the dose to your blood level: 1,000 to 2,000 IU a day suits most people for maintenance, while correcting a diagnosed deficiency may need more under guidance. Because vitamin D is fat-soluble, take it with a meal that contains some fat for better absorption. A D3 plus K2 combination is a sensible extra, and third-party testing from NSF or USP confirms the dose on the label. ## Cautions and the bottom line

Vitamin D is fat-soluble and can build up, so more is not better. Stick to sensible maintenance doses unless a blood test and a clinician direct otherwise, since very high intakes over time can raise blood calcium. If you take medication or have a kidney or parathyroid condition, check first. For everyday mood support the practical answer is simple: pick D3, match the dose to your level, and retest rather than guessing.

Supplements support healthy vitamin D levels; they are not a treatment for depression or any diagnosed condition. If your mood is low, please use [get help](/get-help/).
