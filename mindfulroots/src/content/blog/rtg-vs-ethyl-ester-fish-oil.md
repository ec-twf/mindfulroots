rTG and ethyl ester fish oil deliver the same EPA and DHA, so the choice is mostly about how well each is absorbed and how you take it. The re-esterified triglyceride (rTG) form is absorbed somewhat better in head-to-head studies, which makes it the safer pick if you swallow your capsules on an empty stomach or with a light breakfast. A well-made ethyl ester product is fine for many people, especially taken with a fatty meal, which closes most of the difference and costs less.

Because both forms carry the same omega-3s, the useful question is what you are paying the premium for. Our [omega-3 fish oil page](/products/omega-3-fish-oil/) and the [low-mood guide](/guides/low-mood/) cover where these fats sit before the form debate begins.

## Same omega-3s, two chemical backbones

Fish oil is concentrated to raise its EPA and DHA content, and that concentration step is where the two forms diverge. In the ethyl ester (EE) form, each omega-3 fatty acid is bound to an ethanol molecule, which is efficient to produce and keeps costs down. The re-esterified triglyceride (rTG) form takes that concentrate and re-attaches the fatty acids to a glycerol backbone, the same three-legged structure omega-3s have in whole fish. Once your gut digests either one, the EPA and DHA that reach your blood are identical molecules.

The practical consequence is digestion speed. The triglyceride backbone is what your digestive enzymes are built to handle, so rTG tends to be broken down and absorbed a little more readily. The ethyl ester form needs more help, and that help is dietary fat. This is why the same milligram count on two labels does not always mean the same amount reaching your cells.

## How rTG and ethyl ester compare

| Ethyl ester (EE) | Ethanol-bound fatty acids | Lower on its own, close with fat | A high-fat meal | Lower per bottle |

The table shows the honest picture. The rTG form has the absorption edge, but the edge is conditional. Feed an ethyl ester product a fatty meal and the difference narrows enough that many people will not notice it in their omega-3 index. We leave exact prices out because they shift by concentration and retailer, and a made-up figure would be less useful than none.

## What the studies actually found

A 2010 comparison by [Dyerberg and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.plefa.2010.06.007) measured several fish oil formulations and reported that the re-esterified triglyceride form raised blood omega-3 more than the ethyl ester form over two weeks, with EE sitting below even natural triglyceride fish. A longer trial by [Neubronner and colleagues (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1038/ejcn.2010.239) found rTG improved the omega-3 index faster than EE over six months, though both rose. The meal effect is just as well documented: an earlier study by [Lawson and Hughes (1988)](https://doi.org/10.1016/0006-291X(88)90513-2) showed ethyl ester absorption climbed sharply when taken with a high-fat meal rather than a low-fat one. The [NIH Office of Dietary Supplements omega-3 fact sheet](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-HealthProfessional/) treats both forms as legitimate sources of EPA and DHA.

## What to look for in an rTG or triglyceride fish oil supplement

Read the back of the label before the front. The number that matters is total EPA and DHA per serving in milligrams, because "1,000 mg fish oil" can hide a much smaller omega-3 dose. If you take fish oil without much fat, or first thing in the morning, choose a product that states the triglyceride or rTG form, since that is where the absorption advantage earns its keep. If you reliably take it with a fatty meal, a concentrated ethyl ester product delivers the same omega-3s for less. In both cases prioritise third-party purity testing, such as an IFOS certification, so you know the oil is low in oxidation and contaminants. Our [omega-3 fish oil page](/products/omega-3-fish-oil/) lays out the EPA and DHA criteria and typical doses, and the [low-mood guide](/guides/low-mood/) shows how these fats fit a broader routine. A stated EPA and DHA total with a purity certificate tells you more than the word "triglyceride" alone.

## When the premium is not worth it

If you always take your fish oil with a real meal that contains some fat, the ethyl ester form largely catches up, and paying extra for rTG buys little you will notice. The premium is worth it mainly when you take capsules on an empty stomach, when you want the highest omega-3 index for the fewest capsules, or when digestion is a known problem for you. Buying rTG and then swallowing it with black coffee and nothing else wastes part of what you paid for, because absorption of any form improves with dietary fat. Form is a smaller lever than dose and freshness: a fresh, well-tested ethyl ester at an honest EPA and DHA dose beats a stale premium oil.

## Cautions

Fish oil is generally well tolerated, with fishy aftertaste and mild digestive upset the most common complaints. High doses can have a mild blood-thinning effect, so if you take an anticoagulant such as warfarin, or you are due for surgery, talk to your prescriber before adding a high-dose product. People with a fish or shellfish allergy should be cautious. Omega-3s support mood and general health; they do not treat depression or any diagnosed condition. If you take prescription medication or manage a health condition, check with your prescriber or pharmacist before starting, whichever form you choose.
