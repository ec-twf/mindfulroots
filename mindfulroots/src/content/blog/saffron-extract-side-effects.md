Saffron extract is a concentrated preparation from the stigmas of the *Crocus sativus* flower, standardised for compounds such as crocin and safranal and studied mainly as a support for low mood. Anyone considering it for daily use is right to ask about side effects first, because "well studied for mood" is not the same as "risk free." The reassuring headline is that at the doses used in research, saffron is generally well tolerated, but the side effects are real, they climb with the dose, and a few groups should not take it at all.

Before getting into the risks, it helps to know the researched context, so our [saffron extract page](/products/saffron-extract/) and the [low mood guide](/guides/low-mood/) cover what the evidence supports and the doses that were actually tested.

## What the studies report at normal doses

Most saffron mood trials use about 30 mg a day, and at that level the side-effect profile is mild. A safety evaluation by [Modaghegh and colleagues (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.phymed.2008.06.003) gave healthy volunteers saffron tablets and found blood counts, and vital signs stayed within normal ranges at moderate doses, with only minor changes at the higher end tested. Across the mood literature, the complaints that come up most are nausea, reduced or increased appetite, headache, and sometimes mild drowsiness or dry mouth.

Systematic reviews reach a similar conclusion. A meta-analysis by [Hausenblas and colleagues (2013)](https://doi.org/10.3736/jintegrmed2013056) of saffron for low mood found it performed better than placebo in the trials without a worrying safety signal at the doses used, and a review by [Lopresti and Drummond (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1002/hup.2434) reached the same cautious verdict. The independent summary at [Examine](https://examine.com/supplements/saffron/) agrees that tolerability looks good in the short term, while noting that long-term human safety data are still limited.

## Where the risk actually rises

The important pattern is that saffron's side effects are dose-dependent. Small mood-support amounts are one thing; large amounts are another. Intakes well above the researched range have been associated with vomiting, dizziness, numbness, and bleeding, and very high doses of saffron are considered toxic. There is no evidence that pushing past roughly 30 mg a day buys more benefit, so the higher-dose risks come with no upside.

The second concern is bleeding and blood thinning. Because saffron may affect clotting, combining it with anticoagulant or antiplatelet medication, or taking it around surgery, is a theoretical risk worth clearing with a clinician. And because saffron appears to nudge serotonin, stacking it with an SSRI or another serotonergic drug is an interaction to raise with your prescriber rather than test yourself.

## Who should skip saffron extract

Some people should not take saffron at all, or only under medical supervision:

- Anyone who is pregnant, because saffron can stimulate the uterus at higher amounts.
- Anyone breastfeeding, where safety is not established.
- People with bipolar disorder, since mood-active supplements can be destabilising.
- People taking antidepressants, blood thinners, or blood-pressure medication.
- Anyone with a bleeding disorder or an upcoming operation.

If you fall into one of these groups, the safe move is to talk to a clinician before starting rather than trying a low dose and hoping.

## What to look for in a saffron extract supplement for daily use

If you have cleared saffron with your clinician and want to use it daily, the label is where safety and quality meet. Choose a standardised extract that states the dose per serving in the researched range, around 28 to 30 mg a day, so you are not guessing. Look for a named standardisation, such as a set percentage of safranal or crocin, because that is what ties a product to the trials. Prioritise third-party testing for purity: saffron is one of the most adulterated botanicals on the market, often padded with cheaper plant material or dye, and testing is your protection against that. Our [saffron extract page](/products/saffron-extract/) covers the evidence and typical use, and the [low mood guide](/guides/low-mood/) shows where it sits among other supports. A clear dose and a real standardisation figure tell you far more than a "pure" claim on the front of the jar.

## When to see a doctor

Stop taking saffron and seek advice if you notice unusual bruising or bleeding, a rash or swelling that suggests an allergic reaction, persistent nausea or vomiting, or any sharp change in mood. Saffron supports mood; it does not treat depression or any diagnosed condition, and it is not a substitute for prescribed care. If your low mood is persistent or getting worse, talk to a clinician rather than relying on a supplement.
