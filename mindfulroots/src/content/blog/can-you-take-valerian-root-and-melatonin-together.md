Valerian root (*Valeriana officinalis*) is a sedative herb whose root has been used for centuries to ease restlessness and support sleep. The common question is whether it is safe to combine with melatonin, the hormone your brain releases as darkness falls to signal that night has come. For most healthy adults the two are generally considered a gentle pairing, because they reach the same goal by different routes rather than piling onto one pathway. Both still push you toward drowsiness, and a few groups should check with a clinician before trying them together.

If your aim is simply better sleep, valerian and melatonin are not the only options. A well-tolerated mineral like [magnesium glycinate](/products/magnesium-glycinate/) supports the same wind-down process, and our [sleep guide](/guides/sleep/) shows how these supports compare so you can start with one rather than stacking several.

## How each one works

Melatonin is a timing signal, not a sedative in the way a sleeping pill is. Your pineal gland releases it in the evening to tell the body that night has arrived, which helps set the circadian rhythm. A meta-analysis by [Ferracioli-Oda and colleagues (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0063773) found that supplemental melatonin modestly shortened the time to fall asleep and improved sleep quality across primary sleep disorders. The [NCCIH overview of melatonin](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/melatonin-what-you-need-to-know) makes the same point: it is most useful for shifting timing, such as jet lag, rather than as a nightly knockout.

Valerian works on a different lever. Its compounds, including valerenic acid, are thought to increase activity at GABA receptors, the calming signal in the nervous system, which is a route closer to how some sedatives act. A systematic review by [Bent and colleagues (2006)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amjmed.2006.02.026) concluded that valerian may improve subjective sleep quality, though the trials were small and uneven. The [NCCIH page on valerian](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/valerian) reaches a similar cautious verdict: some people find it helpful, but the evidence is not strong.

## Why the pairing needs a little care

The reason this combination rarely raises a red flag is that melatonin and valerian do not compete for the same receptor in a way that stacks one effect to a risky level. Melatonin adjusts your internal clock. Valerian eases the physical and mental tension that keeps many people awake. That is different from taking two serotonin-raising agents at once, where the effects genuinely add up on a single pathway and the risk climbs.

The real caution here is additive sedation. Because valerian nudges the GABA system and melatonin lowers your alertness, taking enough of both can leave you groggier than you intended, and that grogginess can carry into the morning. It matters most if you also drink alcohol, take a prescription sedative or benzodiazepine, or need to drive early. Start with the lowest dose of each, keep them well clear of any other sedating substance, and give yourself a full night before judging the result.

## What a sensible trial looks like

If you want to test the pairing, keep it short and simple. A common melatonin dose is 0.5 to 3 mg taken 30 to 60 minutes before bed, and more is not better, since higher doses leave some people foggy. Valerian is usually taken as a standardised root extract in the evening. Try one supplement on its own for several nights first, so you can tell which one actually helps, then add the second only if you still need it. That staged approach keeps the morning-after heaviness in check and stops you guessing about which one did the work.

## Cautions and interactions

Melatonin can interact with blood thinners, blood-pressure medication, immunosuppressants, diabetes medication, and some seizure treatments, so it is not risk-free just because it sits on a shop shelf. Valerian can add to the effect of sedatives, benzodiazepines, and alcohol, and it may slow the breakdown of some medications in the liver. Avoid both in pregnancy and breastfeeding, and do not give either to a child without a clinician's guidance. If you take any prescription medication, manage a health condition, or find you need these supplements night after night, talk to your prescriber or pharmacist before combining them. Persistent insomnia is worth investigating rather than masking.
