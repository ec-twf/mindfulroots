If you want help with sleep, stress or simply a gentle way to top up a low magnesium intake, magnesium glycinate is the sensible default: it absorbs well and rarely upsets the stomach. Magnesium threonate is the form built to get magnesium into the brain, so it is the one to consider specifically for memory and focus, but most of that evidence is still from animals and it costs far more per dose.

Both carry the same mineral, so this is a question of delivery and goal, not of one being a stronger magnesium. You can see the spec on the [magnesium glycinate page](/products/magnesium-glycinate/), and the [sleep guide](/guides/sleep/) sets it among other options if calm nights are the aim.

## Same mineral, different carrier

Magnesium never travels alone in a supplement. It is bound to another molecule that changes how it is absorbed and where it tends to go. In glycinate, magnesium is attached to glycine, an amino acid that is itself mildly calming and easy on the gut. In threonate, it is bound to L-threonic acid, a compound chosen because it appeared to move magnesium across the blood-brain barrier more readily. That single design difference is the whole story: glycinate is optimised for tolerability and general use, threonate for brain delivery.

## What the evidence actually shows

The threonate story starts in the lab. A 2010 study raised brain magnesium in rats and improved learning and memory using magnesium L-threonate, where ordinary magnesium forms did not shift brain levels much ([Slutsky et al., 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20152124/)). A later human trial in older adults with mild cognitive complaints reported improvements over 12 weeks, but it was small and industry-linked ([Liu et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.3233/JAD-160139)). That is genuinely interesting, but it is one modest trial resting on a lot of animal data.

Glycinate has no comparable brain-delivery claim, and it does not need one. The everyday case for magnesium rests on correcting low intake and on magnesium's role in the stress and sleep systems. A systematic review found magnesium supplementation was associated with modest reductions in subjective anxiety ([Boyle et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu9050429)), and an older trial in elderly people with insomnia reported better sleep measures ([Abbasi et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23853635/)). For the numbers on requirements and safe upper limits, the [NIH Office of Dietary Supplements magnesium sheet](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Magnesium-HealthProfessional/) is the reference.

## What to look for in a magnesium glycinate supplement

The single most useful habit is to read the elemental magnesium figure, not the compound weight. A label boasting "1000 mg magnesium glycinate" may deliver only around 140 mg of actual magnesium, and that elemental number is what your body counts. For a glycinate product, look for magnesium bisglycinate that states the elemental dose per serving, ideally in the 100 to 200 mg range so you can build up gently, plus third-party testing and few fillers. If cognition is your reason to buy, the brain-targeted option is magnesium L-threonate (sold as Magtein), where the studied intake is around 1.5 to 2 grams of the compound daily split across the day. Whichever you choose, keep your total daily magnesium sensible to avoid loose stools. ## Cautions

Magnesium is generally safe from food and supplements at reasonable doses, but too much from supplements causes diarrhoea and cramping, and very high intakes matter more if your kidneys do not clear magnesium well. If you have reduced kidney function, or take medicines that magnesium can bind and blunt (some antibiotics, thyroid medication, bisphosphonates), space the doses and check with your prescriber first. Supplements support a healthy routine; they are not a substitute for medical care.
