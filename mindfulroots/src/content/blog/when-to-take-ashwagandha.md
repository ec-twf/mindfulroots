There is no single correct time to take ashwagandha, so match it to your goal. Most stress research used it twice a day with meals, which makes a morning-and-evening split a sensible default. If your main goal is sleep, one evening dose fits better. Either way, take it with food and keep the timing consistent, because steady daily use matters far more than the exact hour.

If you want daytime calm and cortisol support, splitting the dose across breakfast and dinner mirrors the trials most closely. If you mainly want help winding down, put the dose in the evening. ## What ashwagandha is and why timing is flexible

Ashwagandha (*Withania somnifera*) is an adaptogenic herb used to support the body's response to stress. It is standardised for compounds called withanolides, and its main studied effect is a gradual lowering of the stress hormone cortisol alongside improvements in perceived stress and sleep. It works by nudging the stress-response system over weeks rather than producing an immediate calming hit.

That slow, cumulative mechanism is exactly why the timing is forgiving. Unlike a fast-acting sedative, ashwagandha does not need to be taken at a precise moment to work. What builds the benefit is a steady daily level, so the practical questions are how to split the dose and how to take it comfortably, not whether 8am or 9pm is objectively better.

## Matching the timing to your goal

**For daytime stress and cortisol: split the dose.** Several trials that reported lower cortisol and perceived stress used a twice-daily schedule of a standardised root extract, taken with meals ([Salve et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.6466); [Lopresti et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1097/MD.0000000000017186)). Morning and evening with food is therefore the most evidence-aligned default for general stress support.

**For sleep: take it in the evening.** A controlled trial in adults with insomnia found that ashwagandha root extract improved sleep quality and sleep onset over several weeks ([Langade et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.5797)). If sleep is your priority, concentrating the dose in the evening with dinner lines the effect up with when you want it.

**For consistency: pick a time you will keep.** An earlier trial reporting a marked cortisol reduction dosed the extract daily over 60 days ([Chandrasekhar et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23439798/)). Across these studies the common thread is not a magic hour but daily adherence over one to two months.

Ashwagandha sits in the moderate-evidence tier for stress and sleep: the trials are reasonably designed but often small and industry-linked, so treat the timing advice as practical guidance rather than a strict rule.

## What to look for in an ashwagandha supplement to take at night

For an evening dose aimed at sleep and stress, the label is where the decision is made. Check four things. First, a root extract standardised to its withanolide content, with that percentage and the dose per serving stated, so you can match the amounts trials used. Second, third-party testing for identity and purity, which is worth having for any botanical. Third, a single clear dose you can take with your evening meal, rather than a proprietary blend that hides the amounts. Fourth, a form you will take every day, since the benefit depends on consistency.

## Cautions and interactions

Ashwagandha can lower blood sugar and blood pressure and may increase thyroid hormone levels, so people taking diabetes, blood-pressure, thyroid, or sedative medication should check with a clinician first. It may stimulate immune activity, which is a concern with autoimmune conditions, and it should be avoided in pregnancy. Taking it with food reduces the mild stomach upset some people notice.

Ashwagandha supports the body's stress response and sleep; it does not treat anxiety disorders, depression, or insomnia, and it is not a substitute for care. If stress or sleep problems persist, speak with a clinician. See [/get-help/](/get-help/) for support options.
