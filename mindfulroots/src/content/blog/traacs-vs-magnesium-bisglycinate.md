If you are choosing between "TRAACS" and plain magnesium bisglycinate, they are not two different minerals. TRAACS is a branded, certified version of the same glycine-bound magnesium, so the real choice is between a generic chelate of unknown quality and one whose chelation is documented. For most people, a generic that clearly states its elemental dose and shows third-party testing is fine; TRAACS earns its premium only when a label is otherwise vague about whether the chelate is real. Our [magnesium glycinate page](/products/magnesium-glycinate/) covers the whole ingredient before brand names enter the picture.

Because this is a manufacturing and labelling distinction rather than a contest between two compounds, the useful question is not "which magnesium wins" but "does the label prove I am getting a fully reacted chelate". That is the one thing a buyer cannot see through the bottle, and it is where the branded grade and a good generic actually diverge.

## Same molecule, different paperwork

Magnesium bisglycinate is magnesium bonded to two molecules of the amino acid glycine. That bond, called a chelate, is why the form is easy on the stomach: glycine-bound magnesium is absorbed partly through amino acid and dipeptide transport rather than depending entirely on stomach acid to release a free mineral. An older ileostomy study by [Schuette and colleagues (1994)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7815675/) showed magnesium delivered as a glycine chelate was absorbed through this peptide route.

TRAACS is a trademark, not a molecule. It stands for "The Real Amino Acid Chelate System" and is Balchem's (formerly Albion's) line of certified mineral chelates. Buying TRAACS Magnesium Bisglycinate Chelate means the manufacturer certifies the chelate is fully reacted and states the elemental magnesium per gram. Buying an unbranded "magnesium bisglycinate" means you are trusting that the same is true, which it often is, and sometimes is not.

## Where a generic falls short

The gap the branded grade closes is real but narrow. A poorly made generic can be only partly reacted, so some of the magnesium never bonded to glycine and behaves like a plain inorganic salt. Worse, some cheap products labelled "magnesium glycinate" are magnesium oxide sprayed with glycine, or a "buffered" blend that quietly mixes bisglycinate with magnesium oxide to lift the elemental count and cut cost. Magnesium oxide is poorly absorbed. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements [magnesium fact sheet](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Magnesium-HealthProfessional/) notes that forms dissolving well in fluid are more completely absorbed than oxide.

None of this means the branded name is the only safe route. It means the buyer's job is to confirm the chelate is genuine, which TRAACS does on your behalf and a transparent generic does through its own label.

## What the evidence does and does not say

Be careful not to over-read the chelate story. A crossover trial by [Walker and colleagues (2003)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14596323/) actually found magnesium citrate more bioavailable over 60 days than either oxide or an amino acid chelate, so "chelate" is not automatically the top absorber for every outcome. The honest reading is that a genuine bisglycinate is well absorbed and gentle, which suits daily use for sleep and stress support, and that the branded-versus-generic question is about proof of quality, not a large clinical gap. On the outcome most readers care about, a review by [Boyle and colleagues (2017)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28445426/) found the evidence for magnesium easing subjective anxiety promising but still limited, whichever form is used.

## What to look for in a fully chelated magnesium bisglycinate supplement

Judge the label, not the logo. First, find the **elemental magnesium per serving**, not the weight of the compound: a "1000 mg magnesium bisglycinate" capsule may deliver only around 100 to 150 mg of actual magnesium. Second, check the wording says **fully reacted** or **fully chelated**, not merely "bisglycinate", and does not list magnesium oxide unless you specifically want a buffered blend. Third, prefer a stated chelate system such as **TRAACS**, or a generic that is equally specific about being fully reacted. Fourth, insist on **third-party testing** for identity and heavy metals. A daily magnesium dose for adults commonly falls in the 200 to 400 mg elemental range, kept within the tolerable upper intake from supplements. ## When the premium is not worth it

If a generic bisglycinate already states its elemental dose, says it is fully reacted or fully chelated, lists no undisclosed magnesium oxide, and carries third-party testing, the branded chelate gives you almost nothing extra. You would be paying for the trademark, not for a better molecule or a real bump in absorption. The premium is worth it only in the opposite case: a bargain product that names no elemental figure, hides behind the word "bisglycinate" alone, and shows no testing. There, TRAACS certification is buying the one thing you could not otherwise verify. For most careful shoppers, a transparent generic and a TRAACS product perform the same in the body.

## Cautions

Magnesium supplements are well tolerated for most adults, with loose stools the usual sign of too much. People with reduced kidney function should not supplement magnesium without medical advice, because impaired clearance can let levels rise dangerously. Magnesium can also reduce the absorption of some antibiotics and thyroid medication if taken at the same time, so separate them by a few hours. If you take prescription medicine or have a health condition, talk to your prescriber before starting.
