L-tryptophan is an essential amino acid your body uses as the starting material to make serotonin, which it then converts into melatonin, the hormone that signals night to your brain. Melatonin taken as a supplement is that finished hormone in a bottle. So when people ask whether you can take L-tryptophan and melatonin together, they are really asking about combining the front end of a pathway with its end product. For most healthy adults the answer is yes, with a few sensible caveats, and the reason is that the two do different jobs on the same production line.

If your interest is really in the serotonin-precursor side, our [5-HTP hub](/products/5-htp/) is worth reading first, because 5-HTP is the step between tryptophan and serotonin and is the form most sleep and mood supplements actually use. This post stays on the pairing question.

## How the two connect

Your body runs tryptophan through a chain: tryptophan becomes 5-hydroxytryptophan (5-HTP), then serotonin, and at night the pineal gland converts some of that serotonin into melatonin. Supplemental L-tryptophan feeds the top of that chain. Supplemental melatonin skips the chain entirely and delivers the end hormone. Because they enter at different points, they are not competing for the same receptor, which is part of why the combination tends to be well tolerated in healthy people.

That shared pathway is also the source of the one real caution. Anything that pushes serotonin higher carries a theoretical serotonin-syndrome risk when layered onto serotonergic medication, and tryptophan sits squarely in that category.

## What the evidence says

Tryptophan's effect on sleep and mood has been studied for decades. A 2010 review in *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews* summarised how tryptophan loading shifts serotonin availability and, in turn, cognition, mood, and sleep, noting measurable but modest effects ([Silber & Schmitt, 2010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2009.08.005)). Melatonin, meanwhile, has a clearer sleep signal: a 2013 meta-analysis in *PLOS ONE* pooled 19 trials and found it shortened the time to fall asleep and modestly increased total sleep time ([Ferracioli-Oda et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0063773)).

Neither is a heavy hitter on its own, and there is no large trial testing the exact combination for sleep. The plausible logic is that tryptophan supports daytime serotonin tone while melatonin handles the sleep-onset signal at night, but that remains reasoning from mechanism rather than a proven stacked benefit. The U.S. [National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/melatonin-what-you-need-to-know) notes melatonin is generally safe short-term while long-term safety is less established, which is a fair frame for the pair.

## Typical use and timing

Melatonin is usually taken 30 to 90 minutes before your intended sleep time, and lower doses (0.5 to 3 mg) often work as well as larger ones. L-tryptophan is commonly taken in the evening, ideally away from a high-protein meal so it competes less with other amino acids for entry into the brain. People who combine them generally take both in that same pre-sleep window. Start with the lowest melatonin dose that helps and hold the tryptophan modest, so you can tell which one is doing what and avoid stacking two sedating effects into next-morning grogginess.

## Cautions and interactions

The serotonin-syndrome caution is the one that matters most. Because L-tryptophan and 5-HTP raise serotonin activity, combining them with **SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs, triptans, tramadol, or other serotonergic drugs** can push serotonin too high, a potentially dangerous reaction ([Boyer & Shannon, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMra041867)). Melatonin has its own interactions, including with blood thinners, blood-pressure medication, diabetes medication, and immunosuppressants. Older L-tryptophan supplements were also linked to an eosinophilia-myalgia outbreak in 1989 traced to a contaminated batch, which is why third-party-tested products matter. If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, or taking any prescription medication, talk to your prescriber before combining these.
