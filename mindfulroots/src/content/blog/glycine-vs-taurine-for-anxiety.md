If your anxious feelings peak at night and wreck your sleep, glycine is the better first try; if you feel wired and over-stimulated in the day, taurine is the one to test. Both are mild amino acids that support the body's own calming systems, and neither is a treatment for an anxiety disorder. They act on different receptors, which is why the choice comes down to *when* you feel worst. Glycine is the simplest amino acid and doubles as an inhibitory neurotransmitter, meaning it quiets nerve signalling in the brain and spinal cord. Taurine is a conditionally essential amino acid concentrated in the brain and heart that acts on the same calming channels as GABA. That shared destination, the brain's inhibitory system, is why both get talked about for anxiety, but the routes and the human evidence behind them differ.

## How each one works

Glycine binds its own inhibitory receptors and also modulates NMDA receptors, and in sleep studies it produces a small drop in core body temperature that mirrors the natural pre-sleep signal. Taurine acts mainly as an agonist at GABA-A and glycine receptors, the brain's principal "brake" channels, which is the proposed basis for its steadying, non-sedating feel. A review of taurine in the nervous system by [Jakaria and colleagues (2019)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30877926/) describes this receptor activity and taurine's role as a neuromodulator and osmoregulator.

## What the human evidence shows

The evidence is uneven, and it is worth being plain about that. Glycine's clearest human data is about sleep: a controlled study summarised by [Bannai and Kawai (2012)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22293292/) found that 3 g of glycine before bed improved subjective sleep quality and reduced next-day fatigue, and later work by [Kawai and colleagues (2015)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25533534/) traced the sleep-promoting and temperature-lowering effect to NMDA receptors in the brain's clock region. Taurine's anti-anxiety evidence is mostly preclinical, mainly steadying effects in animal models, with human trials still thin, as the [Examine taurine overview](https://examine.com/supplements/taurine/) notes. The [Examine glycine overview](https://examine.com/supplements/glycine/) reaches a similar practical read for glycine: best supported for sleep quality.

## What to look for in a glycine supplement for anxiety

Because neither amino is a licensed treatment, the useful question is which product matches the evidence. For **glycine**, plain glycine powder or capsules at around 3 g before bed matches the dose used in the sleep studies; the powder has a mildly sweet taste and dissolves easily. For **taurine**, single-ingredient products at 500–2,000 mg let you control the dose rather than relying on the small amounts hidden in energy drinks. In both cases, choose a brand that publishes third-party testing for identity and contaminants, and avoid proprietary "calm" blends that bury the dose. ## Cautions and who should be careful

Both amino acids are well tolerated for most healthy adults, with occasional mild stomach upset the most common complaint. Glycine at high doses may add to the effect of sedatives; taurine is generally low-risk but has less long-term human data. Anyone who is pregnant or breastfeeding, has kidney or liver problems, or takes prescription medication, including anything for mood, sleep, or blood pressure, should check with a prescriber before starting either. Persistent or worsening anxiety is a reason to speak to a clinician rather than to raise a supplement dose. Talk to your prescriber before combining any of these with a medicine.
